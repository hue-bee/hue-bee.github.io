Original Nonfiction Passage · Part 1

When a City Listens to Its Own Heat

[1] On a July afternoon, two nearby blocks can feel like different climates. One may be shaded by mature trees; the other may be bordered by dark roofs and wide asphalt lots. Citywide weather stations rarely capture such small differences because their sensors are spaced far apart. To reveal heat at street level, several cities have begun inviting residents to collect temperature readings themselves.

[2] The method looks simple: volunteers mount calibrated sensors on bicycles or cars and travel assigned routes at the same times of day. Yet coordination matters. If one group measures at noon and another after a thunderstorm, the readings cannot be compared fairly. Organizers therefore standardize routes, times, sensor height, and travel speed. They also repeat measurements, preventing a single idling bus or patch of shade from distorting the map.

[3] Community mapping produces more than a colorful picture. A city may discover that two neighborhoods with similar air temperatures have very different exposure patterns. In one, residents can reach shaded parks; in another, people waiting for buses stand beside six lanes of sun-heated pavement. Such details can guide choices about tree planting, reflective roofs, cooling centers, and bus shelters.

Original Nonfiction Passage · Part 2

Data Need Both Precision and Trust

[4] The approach has limits. Volunteers may unintentionally skip streets, and handheld sensors cannot replace long-term scientific stations. More importantly, a heat map alone cannot decide which project should receive funding. It can, however, expose a question that an average temperature hides: who experiences the hottest conditions, for how long, and with what protection nearby?

[5] The strongest projects treat residents not merely as data collectors but as interpreters. After routes are completed, participants examine the map alongside transit schedules, tree inventories, and local knowledge. A red patch on a map might mark a warehouse district with few pedestrians, while a slightly cooler patch might surround a crowded playground. Numbers identify patterns; people explain their significance.

[6] That partnership changes how evidence moves through a city. Instead of arriving as a report produced elsewhere, the findings begin with observations residents helped gather and test. The result is not automatically better policy. It is a clearer conversation about where protection is needed—and why.

Solution Clinic · Item 14

Weighted Mean: Turn Every Average Into a Total

1
Old group total: 12 × 78. Combined total: 20 × 84.
2
Let m be the new group’s mean. Its total is 8m. Write 12(78) + 8m = 20(84).
3
Isolate the new group total first: 8m = 20(84) − 12(78). Now divide by 8 and select the matching choice.
Finish the arithmetic yourself; the deck does not reveal the final answer.
Solution Clinic · Item 17

Counting With a Constraint: Build a Bundle

1
Temporarily tape the two math books together. The shelf now holds one bundle plus four other books: five objects.
2
Arrange those five objects in 5! ways.
3
Inside the bundle, the two books can switch order in 2! ways. Evaluate 5! × 2! and choose the match.
The setup is the explanation; you complete the value.
Solution Clinic · Item 20

Tank Problems: Separate Amount From Rate

1
Starting amount: 0.30(900). Missing amount: 900 − 0.30(900).
2
Net filling rate: 18 − 6 liters per minute. The leak subtracts every minute.
3
Time equals missing amount divided by net rate: [900 − 0.30(900)] ÷ (18 − 6). Estimate, compute, and select.
Do not combine liters with liters per minute until the equation is clear.
