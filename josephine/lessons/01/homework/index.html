Part C — ELA: Paragraph Jobs & Author's Purpose

Passage 1 · 6 Questions
Passage 1: The Habit of Checking Your Work

¶1 Every year, thousands of students lose points on standardized tests not because they lack the skill to solve a problem, but because they never look back at their own answer. A wrong answer, once written down, is treated as final — a decision made and abandoned. Yet the single habit that separates high scorers from everyone else is not raw ability. It is the habit of checking.

¶2 Checking is not the same as rereading. Rereading a solution tends to confirm whatever mistake was already made, because the eye sees what it expects to see. In contrast, a true check uses an independent method — estimating the size of the answer before solving, reversing the operation after solving, or substituting the answer back into the original problem. Each method attacks the problem from a different angle, which is exactly why errors that survive a first pass rarely survive a second.

¶3 Consider a student who mismanages a decimal point and gets an answer ten times too large. A reread will not catch this, because the arithmetic steps, followed correctly on paper, look identical. However, an estimate — a quick mental approximation — would immediately flag the answer as unreasonable. This is why tutors so often ask, before any calculation begins, "About how big should this answer be?"

¶4 Some students resist checking because it feels like doing the problem twice. As a result, they skip it under time pressure, precisely when checking matters most. But a check rarely takes as long as the original solution. Reversing an operation is often a single line of arithmetic. Plugging a value back into an equation is frequently faster than the algebra used to find it in the first place.

¶5 The deepest benefit of checking is not the caught error itself, but the diagnosis it makes possible. A student who checks and finds a mismatch must ask why the two methods disagree — and that question, asked honestly, is what turns a careless slip into a lesson learned, rather than a habit repeated on the next ten problems.

Part C Continued — ELA: Careless vs. Conceptual Errors

Passage 2 · 6 Questions
Passage 2: Two Kinds of Wrong

¶1 Not all wrong answers are created equal. A student who forgets to carry a negative sign has made a different kind of error than a student who never learned how negative numbers behave in the first place — even though both students end up with the same incorrect answer on the page.

¶2 Educators call the first kind of mistake a careless error: a slip that occurs despite the student possessing the necessary knowledge. Consequently, careless errors are usually fixed quickly, often by nothing more than slowing down or adopting a check-your-work habit. The second kind, a conceptual error, is far more stubborn. It signals a genuine gap in understanding that will resurface, in slightly different disguises, until it is directly retaught.

¶3 The trouble is that careless and conceptual errors look identical from the outside. Both produce a red X on a worksheet. In contrast to a doctor diagnosing an illness, a tutor cannot simply glance at the wrong answer and know which kind of error occurred; the diagnosis requires asking the student to explain their reasoning aloud.

¶4 This is precisely why an error log — a simple table where a student records what went wrong on every missed problem — is so valuable. Writing the sentence "I forgot to convert units" is a very different confession than writing "I don't know how to convert units," even though both sentences might describe the same missed problem on the surface.

¶5 Ultimately, the goal of any diagnostic session is not simply to correct the answer, but to correctly label the error. A student and tutor who misdiagnose a conceptual gap as mere carelessness will "fix" the mistake for one afternoon, only to watch it return the following week in a new form.
