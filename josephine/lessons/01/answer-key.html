Zone 2 — Paragraph-Job Annotation

Problems 1–3

Big idea: every paragraph has a JOB, not just a topic. Before you touch a question, write a 3–6 word label for what the paragraph is doing — introducing, contrasting, rebutting, concluding — not what it's about.

Problem 1 L1

A paragraph opens with "Many scientists once believed the Earth was flat." In 3–6 words, what job is this paragraph most likely doing?

1. Read the opener as a signal, not a fact: a statement about an old, disproven belief is rarely the whole point — it's almost always a setup. 2. Ask what usually follows a "people used to think" opener: a correction, a contrast, or new evidence that overturns it. 3. Name the job: "introduces an outdated/mistaken belief."
Answer: Introduces an outdated belief (to be corrected)
Clever move: Any sentence starting with "many people/scientists once believed" is a red flag that the paragraph's real job is set-up-for-a-turn, not stating settled fact.
Problem 2 L2

A paragraph says "However, recent studies suggest the opposite is true." Name its job — and explain how the word "however" gave it away.

1. Isolate the transition word: "however" is a contrast signal — it promises the paragraph will push against what came before. 2. Read the rest of the sentence for direction: "the opposite is true" confirms the paragraph is overturning, not just adding to, the prior claim. 3. Name the job: "contradicts/rebuts the previous paragraph's claim."
Answer: Contradicts the previous claim; clue word = "however"
SHSAT trap: Transition words ARE the job label in disguise. "However," "but," "yet," and "in contrast" almost always mark a rebuttal or contrast paragraph — train your eye to catch them first.
Q3.A nonfiction passage's final paragraph restates the author's claim and adds "so what should be done about it." Which job label fits best? L4
A. Introduces a new counter-argument
✓B. Summarizes and calls for action
C. Provides a neutral historical fact
D. Defines a technical term
Why B? "Restates the author's claim" is a summary move, and "what should be done about it" is a call to action — together those two clues point straight at B. A is wrong because a final paragraph restating the SAME claim isn't introducing anything new; C and D describe neutral or definitional jobs with no urgency, but "what should be done" signals persuasion, not neutrality.
SHSAT trap — Topic vs. Job: "What is this paragraph about" is not the same as "what is this paragraph doing." A paragraph can be about bees but its JOB is to contradict the previous paragraph's claim about them. Always answer the second question, not the first.

Zone 3 — Careless vs. Conceptual Autopsy

Problems 4–6

Every wrong answer has a cause. Ask: "If I explained the rule to myself right now, could I re-solve this from scratch?" Yes means careless (a slip in execution); no means conceptual (the rule itself was missing).

Problem 4 L1

A student wrote 3.5 ft = 350 in when converting feet to inches (should be 42 in). Careless or conceptual? Name the exact cause.

1. Check the rule itself: 1 ft = 12 in, so 3.5 ft = 3.5 × 12 = 42 in. 2. Diagnose the wrong answer: 350 looks like the student multiplied by 100 instead of 12 — a wrong conversion factor, not a missing concept about what "convert" means. 3. Classify: the student knew a conversion was needed but grabbed the wrong factor (12 vs. 100) — that's an execution slip.
Answer: Careless — used the wrong conversion factor (likely confused with a metric ×100 habit)
Clever move: When a wrong answer is suspiciously "round" (350 instead of 42), suspect a swapped conversion factor before assuming the whole concept is missing.
Problem 5 L2

Solve 4.2 − 1.75. If a student lined up the decimals wrong and got 4.15, is that careless or conceptual? Then find the correct answer.

1. Line up decimal points correctly: 4.20 − 1.75. 2. Subtract: 4.20 − 1.75 = 2.45. 3. Diagnose: the student understands subtraction but misaligned place values — a mechanical slip, not a missing rule about what subtraction means.
Answer: Careless (misaligned decimals); correct answer = 2.45
SHSAT trap: Decimal subtraction errors almost always trace back to alignment, not the operation itself. Always pad with a trailing zero (4.20, not 4.2) before subtracting to prevent this exact slip.
Q6.A student converts 2.5 hours to minutes and writes 150 minutes, then adds it to 45 minutes and gets 105 minutes total. Which statement is TRUE? L4
A. Both steps were conceptual errors
✓B. The conversion was correct; the addition was careless (should be 195)
C. The conversion was careless; the addition was conceptual
D. There is no error in either step
Why B? 2.5 × 60 = 150 minutes is correct — that step is clean. But 150 + 45 should equal 195, not 105; the student likely subtracted or dropped a digit while adding, which is a pure execution slip, not a misunderstanding of addition. A and C wrongly label the correct conversion step as an error, and D ignores the clear 195 vs. 105 mismatch.
SHSAT trap — One Problem, Two Error Types: Multi-step problems can be conceptually correct in one step and carelessly wrong in another. Tag EACH step separately in your error log, not just the final answer.

Zone 5 — The Check-Your-Work Rule

Problems 7–9

Never verify the same way twice. A calculation error hides inside its own method — redoing identical steps just repeats the blind spot. Use a second METHOD: estimate, reverse the operation, or plug the answer back in.

Problem 7 L1

A rope is 8.4 meters long. Convert to centimeters, then verify your answer by estimating: is 840 cm roughly 8 meters?

1. Convert: 1 m = 100 cm, so 8.4 m × 100 = 840 cm. 2. Verify with estimation: 840 cm ÷ 100 ≈ 8.4 m, which matches the original 8.4 m.
Answer: 840 cm — estimate check confirms it (840 cm ≈ 8.4 m)
Clever move: Rounding 840 to "about 800" and dividing by 100 to get "about 8" is enough to confirm the decimal placement is right — that's the whole point of an estimate check.
Problem 8 L2

Solve 17 − 5.65. Then verify using the reverse operation: does your answer plus 5.65 return 17?

1. Subtract carefully: 17.00 − 5.65 = 11.35. 2. Reverse-check: 11.35 + 5.65 = 17.00. ✓
Answer: 11.35 — reverse operation confirms it (11.35 + 5.65 = 17)
SHSAT trap: Redoing the SAME subtraction a second time can reproduce the same alignment mistake. Addition is a genuinely different method, which is why it catches errors subtraction alone would miss.
Q9.A recipe needs 2.5 pints of liquid, but the measuring cup only shows ounces (1 pint = 16 oz). A student pours 32 oz and calls it "close enough." Which check method proves this is wrong? L4
✓A. Plug back in: 32 oz ÷ 16 = 2 pints, not 2.5 — short by 8 oz
B. No check is needed since 32 is close to 2.5
C. Estimating alone confirms 32 oz is correct
D. Reverse operation is not applicable to unit conversion
Why A? Plugging 32 oz back into the conversion (32 ÷ 16 = 2 pints) exposes the shortfall immediately: the recipe needs 2.5 pints = 40 oz, so 32 oz is 8 oz short. B and C treat "close enough" as a valid check, which it never is on the SHSAT — eyeballing isn't verification. D is false; reverse operations work perfectly well on unit conversions, as shown here.
SHSAT trap — "Close Enough" Isn't a Check: Eyeballing an answer feels like checking but skips the actual verification step. The mandatory rule is a SECOND calculation, done a different way — not a gut feeling about whether the number looks right.

Tutor Notes — High-Value Habits

  • Paragraph job: label the function (introduce, contrast, rebut, conclude) in 3–6 words before answering any purpose/structure question; watch transition words like "however" as job clues.
  • Topic vs. job: "what it's about" and "what it's doing" are different questions — always answer the second one for purpose/structure items.
  • Careless vs. conceptual: ask "could I re-solve this right now if I explained the rule to myself?" Yes = careless (fix with a habit); No = conceptual (fix by re-teaching).
  • Multi-step problems: tag each step of the solution separately — one step can be conceptually sound while another is a careless slip.
  • Check-your-work: never verify with the identical method twice; use estimation, the reverse operation, or plugging the answer back in.
  • Error log habit: log every miss as Problem | Type | Careless/Conceptual | Cause — this is the evidence base for what gets re-taught later in the season.
