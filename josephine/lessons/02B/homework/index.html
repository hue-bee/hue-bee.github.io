Listening at the Pond

Original informational practice passage · constructed scenario

[¶1] At a neighborhood pond, a student team wanted to learn whether the frog population was growing. They placed a recorder near the water and saved a short sound sample each evening. Recordings seemed less disruptive than searching the reeds, and the students hoped to turn the calls into a reliable count.

[¶2] An unusually loud recording prompted one student to announce that more frogs had arrived. Ecologist Ms. Chen asked the group to listen again. A single frog could call repeatedly, she explained, while several frogs could remain silent. Loudness described the sound reaching the microphone; it did not directly reveal the number of animals producing it.

[¶3] The team began keeping the microphone in the same position and recording at the same time on evenings with similar weather. They also arranged for an observer to count visible frogs during some recording periods. Comparing the two records would help them investigate whether changes in calling reliably tracked changes in the number of frogs.

[¶4] During one visit, a cart rattled along the path, briefly covering the calls. A student marked the interruption rather than recording that interval as silence. Later, the team kept a separate column for sounds that interfered with listening. An empty space in their record could now mean that the evidence was unavailable, not that the pond was empty.

[¶5] These changes did not resolve every uncertainty. Frogs hidden in the reeds could escape the observer, and quiet frogs could escape the recorder. Still, the students could make a more precise claim: their recordings documented calling under stated conditions. A complete population estimate would require additional evidence. The recorder remained useful, but its usefulness depended on knowing what it could—and could not—measure.

The Crooked Row

Original fiction practice passage

[¶1] Theo stretched a string between two stakes and admired the straight edge of the new garden bed. Tomorrow, neighbors would tour the student garden. He imagined their photographs: seedlings in even rows, paths meeting at exact angles. He pulled one stake a little farther until the string stopped trembling.

[¶2] June arrived with the watering cart. At the corner nearest Theo’s bed, its front wheel caught on the path’s edge. She backed up, turned, and tried again. Theo watched the tire press a dark crescent into the soil. “If you go around the other side,” he began, pointing toward the far gate.

[¶3] “That works for this bed,” June said. “What about the next one?” She rested the cart handles on the ground. Theo looked along the row. The path narrowed at every corner. From the gate, his careful angles had looked generous; standing beside the cart, he could see how little room they left.

[¶4] He reached for the string, then hesitated. Moving the stakes would make the row uneven. For a moment he wished June had brought a smaller watering can. Then he pictured carrying that can back and forth through all six beds. He untied the string. The loose end fell across his shoe.

[¶5] By late afternoon, one corner curved outward and another path was wider than its neighbor. Theo brushed soil from a stake as June rolled the cart past without stopping. When a classmate offered to straighten the curve before the tour, Theo shook his head. “Let’s bring the cart through the other paths first,” he said. He stood at the gate again, this time following the route a visitor would take.
