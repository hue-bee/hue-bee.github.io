5–30 minutes · ELA repair

Read the attitude,
not just the event

Tone is the speaker’s stance toward the subject. Imagery is the evidence. Purpose is the job that evidence performs.

The interpretation ladder

Image → implication → attitude → purpose

Precision check
“Sad” names a mood. What sharper word would distinguish disappointment, nostalgia, resignation, and dread?
Tutor model · original poem

“After the Ferry”

The pier keeps one lamp after dawn, its pale coin pressed against the rain. Across the river, windows kindle— small promises I cannot claim. The ferry’s wake unthreads the gray; its silver seam is gone too soon. I turn before the bell has faded, carrying the unfinished tune.
Original instructional text
First read: literal floor
What physically happens? Keep the answer plain; interpretation comes after the scene is stable.
Second read: charged images
Why “one lamp,” “cannot claim,” “gone too soon,” and “unfinished”? What emotional direction do these choices share?
Third read: shift
Does the speaker move toward acceptance, away from hope, or between both? Point to the turn.
Worked model 1

From one image to a defensible reading

“The ferry’s wake unthreads the gray;
its silver seam is gone too soon.”
hopefulmixedregretful
The strongest interpretation must explain both silver and gone too soon.
1

Name the image

A bright line briefly appears in a gray river, then dissolves.

2

Infer the tension

The brightness suggests possibility; its quick disappearance makes that possibility temporary.

3

State the job

The image develops a mixed attitude: the speaker recognizes hope but expects to lose it.

Trap: mood-only reading.
“The poem is sad” ignores the silver brightness. A strong answer preserves the tension instead of flattening it.

Independent locked block · original prose

The Observatory Key

By the time Mara reached the observatory, the caretaker had already turned the brass key in the outer door. He did not remove it. Instead, he rested two fingers on its worn bow, as if the building might change its mind and admit her after all.

“The clouds have settled in,” he said. Above them, the dome looked less like a window onto the heavens than a gray, unblinking eye.

Mara held up the notebook her father had left unfinished. “Clouds move.”

The caretaker’s smile was slight and strangely formal. For thirty years he had opened the dome whenever her father arrived, even on evenings when the forecast promised nothing. Now he studied Mara with the courteous patience one gives a child who has asked to purchase the moon.

Then the wind shifted. A thin break appeared in the west, no wider than a ribbon. The caretaker glanced at the key. Mara said nothing.
Original instructional text · Suggested block time: 7 minutes plus 60-second review

A sign is not decoration. It records order. Every move must preserve which values make the original statement true.

Why the sign flips

Negative multiplication mirrors the number line

Start with a true order
2 < 5. Which point is farther right?
Reflect both points
Multiplying by −1 sends each value to the opposite side of zero.
Preserve truth
−2 > −5. The sign reverses because the spatial order reverses.
Worked model 2

Compound inequality: flip all three parts

Problem
−8 < 2 − 2x ≤ 10
Solve and represent the result on a number line.
Prediction:
The final division is by a negative. Both inequality symbols must reverse.
1

Subtract 2 everywhere

−10 < −2x ≤ 8

2

Divide all three parts by −2

Reverse both signs: 5 > x ≥ −4.

3

Rewrite left to right

−4 ≤ x < 5. Close −4, open 5, shade between.

Graphing check

Endpoint, direction, test point

1 · Endpoint
Where is equality?
Solve for the boundary value before drawing anything.
2 · Circle
Included or excluded?
Use a closed circle for ≤ or ≥; open for < or >.
3 · Direction
Which side works?
Test one easy value from a side; do not trust the symbol’s visual direction alone.
Fast proof
If your result is x ≥ 1, test 2 in the original inequality. Then test 0. One should work and one should fail.

50–60 minutes · cluster transfer

Number theory:
count without overlap

When a problem says “neither,” count the union first. The overlap must be subtracted once.

Inclusion–exclusion

Two groups overlap in the LCM

Count(A or B)
Count(A) + Count(B) − Count(A and B)
Count(neither)
Total − Count(A or B)
Why subtract the overlap?
Which numbers were counted once as multiples of 2 and again as multiples of 3?
Worked model 3

How many integers from 1 to 60 are neither?

Problem
Count the integers from 1 through 60 that are divisible by neither 2 nor 3.
Before calculating
Why is “subtract the multiples of 2, then subtract the multiples of 3” incomplete?
1

Count each group

Multiples of 2: 30. Multiples of 3: 20. Multiples of 6: 10.

2

Count the union once

30 + 20 − 10 = 40 integers are divisible by 2 or 3.

3

Take the complement

60 − 40 = 20 integers are divisible by neither.
