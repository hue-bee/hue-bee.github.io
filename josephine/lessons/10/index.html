Level 5 · Mechanics

What Makes a Level 5 Question Hard?

It hides the doorway.The needed skill is familiar, but the entry move is disguised.
It stacks constraints.Several facts must stay true at once; dropping one produces a tempting result.
It taxes working memory.The interface, wording, and calculation compete for attention.
Ask before solving: What must remain true from the first line to the final click?
“Level 5” is this deck’s training label, not an official NYCPS category.

Separate Solving From Entering

1Solve on paper. Keep the interface out of the reasoning stage.
2Translate the output. Decide decimal, fraction, selected tiles, or ordered placement.
3Audit the entry. Check sign, decimal point, numerator/denominator, and every selected option.
4Commit. One final glance at the prompt; then move forward.

Passage A The Quiet Minute

Target: 9 minutes for passage + 7 itemsBegin with careful speed. Label each paragraph’s job.
Original fiction · paragraphs 1–4

1 Mara had repaired the courthouse clock twice in her imagination before Mr. Vale finally handed her the brass key. From the floor of the tower, the mechanism looked less like a clock than a metal forest: gears layered behind gears, rods vanishing into shadow, the pendulum cutting the air with patient authority.

2 “Listen before you touch,” Mr. Vale said. He folded his arms and waited.

3 Mara wanted instructions, not silence. The town’s noon bell had been striking four minutes late, and below them shopkeepers were already turning their faces toward the tower. She counted the pendulum’s swings. On every seventh swing, a faint scrape interrupted the steady tick.

4 “A bent tooth?” she asked. Mr. Vale only raised an eyebrow. Mara leaned closer. The scrape came from the left, but the nearest gear moved cleanly. She followed the sound past it to a narrow lever whose tip brushed the gear only when the pendulum reached the end of its arc.

Passage A continued

Original fiction · paragraphs 5–8

5 A screw at the lever’s base had loosened. Mara reached for the wrench, then stopped. Tightening the screw would move the lever, but how far? She marked its current position with chalk, turned the screw one quarter rotation, and listened again. The scrape remained, softer now.

6 Another quarter turn erased it. The pendulum swung twenty times without interruption. Mara smiled and lifted the wrench away.

7 “Now the difficult part,” Mr. Vale said.

8 Mara’s smile faded. Then she understood. She waited through sixty swings, watching not only the lever but the neighboring gears and the minute hand above them. The mechanism kept its rhythm. Only after the full minute did Mr. Vale nod. Below, the town bell sounded—not triumphantly, Mara thought, but exactly when it should.

Timed ELA · Block 2

Passage B A Roof That Changes the Street

Target: 9 minutes for passage + 7 itemsReset. Do not carry Block 1’s story logic into this text.
Original nonfiction · paragraphs 1–3

1 On a sunny day, a dark roof absorbs much of the energy that strikes it. The warmed roof then transfers heat to the rooms below and to the surrounding air. Across a neighborhood, thousands of dark surfaces can help create an “urban heat island,” an area that remains warmer than nearby places with more vegetation.

2 A cool roof interrupts this process. Its surface is designed to reflect more sunlight and release absorbed heat efficiently. The idea sounds simple, but measuring its effect requires more than comparing two buildings on one afternoon. Buildings differ in height, insulation, shade, use, and ventilation. Weather changes from day to day.

3 For that reason, researchers often compare temperature and energy data over long periods. They may also pair similar buildings or examine the same building before and after a roof change. These methods do not erase every difference, but they reduce the chance that an unrelated factor will be mistaken for the roof’s effect.

Timed ELA · Block 2

Passage B continued

Original nonfiction · paragraphs 4–6

4 Cool roofs are not a complete heat strategy. A highly reflective roof cannot shade a sidewalk, absorb storm water, or replace a poorly functioning cooling system. Trees, green roofs, insulation, and efficient equipment solve different parts of the problem.

5 Nor is the same design ideal everywhere. In a hot climate, reducing summer heat gain may provide a large benefit. In a colder climate, researchers must also consider whether reflecting winter sunlight changes heating needs. Local weather, roof slope, nearby buildings, and the structure’s use all matter.

6 The strongest case for cool roofs, then, is not that one surface can cool an entire city. It is that a carefully chosen surface can become one useful layer in a larger plan—especially when planners measure the local result instead of assuming it.
