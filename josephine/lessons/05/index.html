Fiction Strategy

Track the Character’s Engine

Think like a director
If you removed every description but kept the character’s want, obstacle, and response, would you still recognize the story? Why might those three pieces matter more than the setting?
WANT → WALL → CHANGE
goal → pressure/obstacle → choice, realization, or new behavior
Clever explanation: the plot is the road; the main idea is the steering.
Events tell you where the character traveled. Choices tell you what the journey meant.
Main Idea Filter

Choose the Sentence That Covers the Whole Movie

Ask the “movie trailer” question
If this answer were the trailer’s one-sentence voice-over, would viewers understand the story’s central struggle and what changed?
Trap 1Topic
“This story is about a school play.” True, but empty: no conflict, motive, or change.
Trap 2Detail
“Mara repairs a blue coat.” Accurate—but only one event, not its meaning.
Trap 3Too broad
“Helping others is always important.” A generic lesson could fit hundreds of stories.
TargetMain Idea

Passage Set A · Part 1

“The Blue Thread” Read for Pressure

The Blue Thread
Tool:
Color:

1Mara had volunteered in the school costume room for three months, long enough to know where every spool of thread belonged and not long enough, she feared, for Ms. Ibarra to trust her with real design work. When the director handed her a faded blue coat and said, “It must be ready before tonight’s rehearsal,” Mara heard an opportunity hiding inside an ordinary repair.

2The coat belonged to the actor playing the mayor, a character who entered only twice. Its torn pocket sagged like a tired mouth. Mara imagined replacing the dull buttons with silver ones and sewing a bright braid around the cuffs. Under the stage lights, everyone would notice. More importantly, Ms. Ibarra would notice.

3As Mara turned the pocket inside out, she found a line of tiny blue stitches beneath the lining. They were uneven but stubbornly secure. A paper tag pinned nearby read, “Spring production, 1998—repair by Elena Ruiz.” Mara recognized her grandmother’s name.

4Her grandmother had once worked backstage at the same school. “A costume should help the audience see the character,” she used to say, “not the person who made it.” Mara had always thought the sentence sounded too modest.

Pause—do not summarize yet
What does Mara want on the surface? What deeper need is hiding underneath? Which phrase shows that need?
Digital annotation
Highlight only evidence that changes your prediction. Suggested marks:
W = want P = pressure T = turn
Prediction checkpoint
Will Mara choose work that gets noticed—or work that serves the play? What would each choice reveal?
Passage Set A · Part 2

“The Blue Thread” Find the Turn

Tool:
Color:

5The rehearsal clock glowed 5:12. The actor needed the coat at 5:30. Mara laid the silver braid beside the blue thread. The braid flashed. The thread nearly disappeared against the cloth.

6She threaded the smallest needle and copied her grandmother’s plain, close stitches. Then she reinforced the pocket’s corner, where the actor would shove a heavy ring of stage keys. There was no time for new buttons. At 5:28, she carried the coat to the wings.

7During the mayor’s entrance, the actor swept his hand into the repaired pocket and pulled out the keys without breaking stride. The audience laughed at his line. No one looked at the pocket.

8After rehearsal, Ms. Ibarra examined the coat. Mara prepared to explain why she had left it almost unchanged. Instead, Ms. Ibarra tugged the pocket firmly and smiled. “Invisible and dependable,” she said. “Tomorrow, help me plan the lead’s jacket.”

9Mara returned the silver braid to its drawer. The little blue stitches were hidden again, but now they did not seem modest. They seemed like a signature written for someone patient enough to look inside.

Tone evidence
Compare “sagged like a tired mouth” with “a signature written for someone patient enough to look inside.” How does the emotional color shift?

“The Unsent Speech” Compress the Story

Tool:

1Amara had revised her speech for Mr. Chen’s retirement celebration until every sentence shone. She wanted the librarian—who had recommended books when she was lonely in sixth grade—to understand exactly how much his kindness had mattered.

2Ten minutes before the program, her tablet froze. The file vanished when she restarted it. Amara stared at the blank screen while the cafeteria filled with families. Without her polished sentences, gratitude felt clumsy.

3When her name was called, she carried the useless tablet to the microphone. Then she noticed Mr. Chen straightening a crooked stack of programs, just as he always straightened books no one else saw.

4Amara put the tablet down. She told the audience about the afternoon Mr. Chen had quietly placed a mystery novel beside her and said, “This heroine also eats lunch alone—at first.” Her voice shook, but Mr. Chen laughed softly.

5Afterward, he thanked her for remembering the small moment. Amara realized that the lost file had not erased the speech. It had only removed the parts standing between the memory and the person who needed to hear it.

Math · 25 Minutes

Two Conditions. One Solution Set.

Compound inequalities are boundary stories: stay inside both gates—or escape through either exit.

AND = intersectionOR = unionnegative division = mirror
Compound Logic

AND Squeezes. OR Spreads.

Think about membership
Can a number belong to both conditions at once? If yes, keep only the overlap. If it may satisfy either condition, keep both regions.
AND = pass both gates
Example: −2 < x ≤ 4
OR = choose either exit
Example: x < −2 or x ≥ 4
Three-Part Inequalities

Use the Conveyor Belt

Why all three parts?
In −3 ≤ 2x + 1 < 9, the middle expression is trapped between two boundaries. What would break if you changed only the middle and right side?
−3 ≤ 2x + 1 < 9
subtract 1 from left, middle, right
−4 ≤ 2x < 8
divide all three parts by +2
−2 ≤ x < 4
Negative conveyor = mirror
If you divide all three parts by a negative, both inequality signs reverse. Then rewrite boundaries from least to greatest.
Compound Practice

Solve, Then Describe the Region

7Level 1

8Level 2

9Level 3
Solve 7 < −2x + 1 ≤ 13. What changes when dividing by −2?
10Level 4
Solve 4x + 1 < −11 or −3x + 2 ≤ −10.
SHSAT trap: stopping before standard form
An answer like −3 > x ≥ −6 may be mathematically true but visually backward. Rewrite it −6 ≤ x < −3 before matching a graph.
Word-Problem Translator

Find the Boundary Language First

Read like a lawyer
Does the boundary value itself count? “At least” and “no more than” include equality. “More than” and “fewer than” exclude it.
Clever explanation: build the fence before counting the sheep.
Write the allowed region first. Only then substitute the cost, distance, temperature, or quantity expression.
Inclusive: at least ≥ · at most ≤ · no fewer than ≥
Exclusive: more than > · fewer than < · below <
Context check
If the variable counts tickets, boxes, or people, use whole numbers—even if the algebra returns decimals.
