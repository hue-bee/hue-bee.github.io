The Brass Compass

1 Every Saturday in October, Leena helped her uncle sort donations in the narrow back room of the Harbor Street Museum. She liked the labels, the cotton gloves, and the quiet certainty that every object had a proper place. The upcoming “Neighborhood Voyages” exhibit, however, needed one centerpiece, and the curator had invited volunteers to recommend it.

2 Leena immediately chose a polished brass compass from a sea captain's trunk. Its lid shone beneath the lamp, and its needle settled north with a confident snap. If the compass occupied the center case, visitors would stop. More important, the curator would know that Leena could recognize something significant.

3 At the bottom of the same trunk lay a cloth notebook swollen by damp air. Its pages held grocery lists, sketches of unfamiliar coastlines, and short entries by the captain's daughter, Rosa. One entry described the night their ship reached New York: while the adults searched the harbor for landmarks, twelve-year-old Rosa calmed her younger brothers by inventing names for the lights along the shore.

4 “The compass guided the ship,” Leena told her uncle, placing it beside the notebook.

5 “Perhaps,” he said. “But which object helps us understand the people aboard it?”

6 Leena disliked the question because it made the easy choice feel incomplete. She imagined the center case gleaming around the compass. Then she imagined a visitor leaning over Rosa's cramped handwriting and hearing, for a moment, a nervous child trying to make a dark harbor less frightening.

7 On Monday, Leena presented both objects. She recommended displaying the open notebook in the center, with the compass nearby to show the route. The curator asked why the less impressive object deserved more space.

8 “The compass tells us where they went,” Leena said. “Rosa tells us what arriving required.”

9 At the exhibit opening, most visitors glanced at the compass before bending over the notebook. Leena watched one girl trace the air above Rosa's sketched shoreline. The brass still caught the light, but it no longer seemed like the only thing worth seeing.

The Empty Measure

1 Niko had practiced the opening of his violin solo until he could play it without looking at the page. At the winter concert, he wanted his older sister in the front row to hear proof that he had finally caught up to her.

2 During rehearsal, the cellist beside him whispered that one measure on her photocopied music was missing. The conductor raised her baton. Niko could have faced forward; the error was not in his part.

3 Instead, he used the few silent beats before the entrance to pencil the missing notes onto the cellist's page. He began his own solo a breath late. The first phrase sounded rushed, though the orchestra stayed together.

4 Afterward, Niko avoided his sister's eyes. She met him backstage and tapped the pencil still tucked behind his ear. “Your entrance can be fixed,” she said. “Knowing when someone else needs your measure—that takes longer to learn.”

5 On the ride home, Niko replayed the rushed phrase once. Then he remembered the cellist entering exactly on time, and the mistake no longer filled the whole concert.
