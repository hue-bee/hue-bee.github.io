ELA · New Strategy

The Purpose Funnel — Big Idea First, Detail Second

Think about it
If someone asked you "why did the author mention rainfall data in paragraph 3," could you answer without first knowing what the WHOLE passage is trying to do? Which question has to come first — "what is this passage's job," or "what is this detail's job"?
The funnel, in order
1) State the passage's overall purpose in ONE sentence. 2) Ask how the cited detail serves that purpose: does it support it, complicate it, contrast with it, or illustrate it? Every correct answer choice restates one of those four relationships — nothing else.
ELA · Passage

Read Once. Then Write the Purpose Sentence.

Instructions
Read straight through, top to bottom, one time. Do not answer any question yet. When you finish, write one sentence: "The purpose of this passage is to ___."
Tool:
Color:

Honeybees are often credited with pollinating most of the world's food crops, and beekeepers are frequently praised as quiet guardians of the food supply. This popular narrative, however, glosses over a more complicated ecological reality. Wild, native bee species — of which there are over 20,000 worldwide — perform a substantial share of pollination that managed honeybee colonies cannot replicate, particularly for crops with flower shapes honeybees struggle to access.

Entomologist Rachel Winfree's field studies compared crop yields on farms bordered by wild habitat against farms without it. Fields near wildflower strips and hedgerows showed higher pollination rates even when honeybee hives were absent entirely, suggesting wild bees were doing meaningful work honeybees were not.

Critics of the "save the bees" campaigns argue that this messaging, while well-intentioned, has concentrated public attention and funding on honeybee conservation at the expense of wild bee habitat protection. Because honeybees are a managed livestock species — not an endangered one — resources spent multiplying hives may do little for the wild species facing the steepest population declines.

None of this diminishes the honeybee's genuine agricultural value; large-scale monoculture farming would struggle without portable managed hives. The point is narrower: a food-security story reduced to a single charismatic species obscures the fuller, messier picture of who is actually doing the pollinating.

Recognizing this complexity does not require abandoning honeybee conservation — it requires expanding the story to include the far more numerous, far less visible pollinators working alongside them.

ELA · Worked Example

Funnel It: Why Mention Rachel Winfree?

The question
"The author most likely includes the details about Rachel Winfree's field studies in paragraph 2 in order to —" First: what's your one-sentence purpose for the whole passage? Now: does Winfree's study support that purpose, complicate it, contrast with it, or illustrate it?
The clever shortcut
Purpose sentence: "to show that the honeybee-only pollination story is incomplete." Winfree's study is EVIDENCE for that exact claim — it proves wild bees pollinate even without honeybees present. That's not contrast (nothing is being opposed) and not illustrate (it's not just an example, it's data). It supports the purpose. You never had to reread the paragraph in detail — you just matched its function to one of four verbs.
The trap this avoids
A wrong choice might say Winfree's study "proves honeybees are unnecessary for farming" — true-sounding, but it overshoots the actual purpose. The passage never claims honeybees are unnecessary, only that they're not the whole story. Match the verb to the PASSAGE's purpose, not to a stronger claim that sounds plausible.
ELA · Practice — Levels 1–2

Funnel These — Purpose Sentence First

1Level 1

2Level 2

If your verb doesn't match your OWN purpose sentence, one of the two is wrong — go back and fix the purpose sentence before changing the verb.
ELA · Practice — Levels 3–4

Harder Funnels — Watch for Overshoot Traps

3Level 3
The final paragraph's claim that "recognizing this complexity does not require abandoning honeybee conservation" serves which purpose relative to paragraph 3's criticism of "save the bees" campaigns? Is the final paragraph agreeing, softening, or reversing paragraph 3?
4Level 4
One answer choice claims paragraph 4 exists "to argue that honeybee conservation should be defunded." Using your purpose sentence, explain in one line why this choice overshoots what the passage actually argues — even though paragraph 4 does criticize honeybee-focused funding.
The overshoot trap, named
Wrong choices often take a true detail and push it one step further than the passage actually goes. If a choice uses a stronger word than the passage does ("defund," "unnecessary," "prove"), suspect overshoot before you suspect yourself.

Zone 2 · Angles From Scratch

Two Lines Cross — Draw It Yourself First

Draw before you read the rule
Draw two straight lines crossing in an X. Label the four angles 1, 2, 3, 4 going around. Angles 1 and 3 sit directly across from each other — do they look equal or different in size? Angles 1 and 2 sit next to each other along the same straight line — what must they add up to, and why?
The two rules this X gives you for free
Vertical angles: equal   ·   Straight-line pair: sum = 180°
Zone 3 · Angle Sums

Why 180°? — Tear the Corners Together

The clever proof, no formula needed
Draw any triangle. Now imagine tearing off its three corners and placing them side by side, tip to tip, along a straight line. If they fit perfectly along that line every single time — no matter the triangle's shape — what does that tell you their sum must equal? Now: a rectangle can be cut along one diagonal into two triangles. What does that make a quadrilateral's angle sum?
Build your own formula sheet
Triangle: 180°   →   Quadrilateral: 2 × 180° = 360°
Zone 3 · Catch the Tutor

Watch the Tutor Solve This — Then Fix It

The tutor's work (on purpose, wrong)
"A triangle has angles of 50°, 60°, and x°. So x = 180 − 50 − 60 = 70°. But then this triangle sits next to a straight angle, so the angle supplementary to x is also 70°." Two answers, both labeled 70° — is that possible, and where exactly did the reasoning break?
The fix, once you say it
The triangle angle sum gives x = 70° correctly. But "supplementary" means adding to 180°, not copying the same value — the tutor confused "supplementary" with "equal." The angle next to x on a straight line is 180 − 70 = 110°, not 70°. Vertical angles are equal; supplementary angles add to 180° — never mix up which rule gives equal vs. which gives a sum.
Zone 4 · Practice — Levels 1–2

Angle Basics — Check by Verifying the Total

1Level 1
Two angles are supplementary. One measures 115°. What is the other? Check: do your two angles add to exactly 180°?
2Level 1
Two angles are complementary. One measures 37°. What is the other? Complementary means the sum is 90° — write that equation before solving.
3Level 2
Two lines cross to form four angles. One angle measures 72°. Its vertical angle measures what? Its adjacent (straight-line) angle measures what? Give both.
4Level 2
A triangle has angles of 42° and 88°. Find the third angle. Write the triangle-sum equation before solving, then verify all three add to 180°.
Zone 4 · Practice — Levels 3–4

Multi-Step Angles — Algebra Meets Geometry

5Level 3
A quadrilateral has angles measuring 90°, 90°, 105°, and x°. Find x. Set up the sum-to-360° equation before isolating x.
6Level 3
Two supplementary angles are represented as 3x and 2x. Find the value of each angle. Write "3x + 2x = 180" first — the algebra only works if the geometry sentence is right.
7Level 4
A triangle's three angles are (x), (x + 10), and (2x − 10). Find x, then find all three angle measures. Which rule sets up your equation — sum to 180, or something else?
8Level 4
Two lines cross. One pair of vertical angles is labeled (4x) and the angle adjacent to it on the same straight line is labeled (2x + 30). Find x, then find all four angles around the intersection.
The one question that sorts every one of these
Are the two angles across from each other (vertical → equal) or next to each other on a straight line (supplementary → sum to 180)? Name the relationship out loud before writing any equation.
Error Log Review

Any Recurring Pattern? — Name It, Don't Just Note It

Vertical vs. Supplementary — Math
If today's practice showed you mixing up "equal" (vertical) with "adds to 180" (supplementary), that's the exact same error family as compounding-vs-addition from Week 1 — a base/relationship confusion, not a facts gap.
Overshoot Traps — ELA
If a purpose question tempted you toward a stronger-sounding answer than the passage supports, log it as "overshoot" — the same trap family as "true but useless," just in disguise.
Why naming the pattern matters
A named trap is a trap you can watch for on the real test. An unnamed mistake just repeats. Two attempts on geometry have shown zero net improvement — naming today's specific slip is what breaks that streak.
