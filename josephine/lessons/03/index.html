Warm-Up · The Diagnosis

Why Structure Questions Feel So Slippery

Think first
If a question asks "how is this passage organized?", do you need to remember EVERY sentence, or just the big JOB each paragraph is doing? What's the smallest amount of information you'd need to answer that question correctly?
Why this matters
Organization/Structure accuracy dropped from 57.14 percent to 14.29 percent — one of the sharpest declines in the whole profile. That's not a knowledge gap; it's a memory-load problem. A structure map fixes it by turning "remember everything" into "read a diagram."
Your Habit Target
Before answering ANY structure question, sketch the map first — even a messy one. The map is the answer key; the passage is just where you found the pieces.
Zone 1 · Structure MapsSharpest Decline

A Structure Map Is a Blueprint of the Passage's Logic

Think about it
A passage opens with a claim, gives one example that seems to disagree, explains why it actually doesn't, then restates the claim. If you had to draw this as 4 boxes with arrows, what would each box say — and would the boxes be about IDEAS or about DETAILS?
Every box gets a JOB, not a summary
Claim → Counter-Example → Rebuttal → Conclusion
Zone 1 · Practice, Set 1

Structure Maps — Easy to Hard

1Level 1
A story goes: introduce character → character faces a problem → character solves it. Sketch this as 3 boxes. What job does the middle box do?
2Level 1
A nonfiction passage: state a problem → describe two failed solutions → describe the solution that finally worked. How many boxes does this map need, and what's in the last one?
3Level 2
A question asks: "Which paragraph most directly sets up the contrast developed in paragraph 4?" Using a structure map, do you scan every paragraph again, or check which earlier box the arrow to Box 4 comes from?
4Level 2
A passage has 6 paragraphs, but paragraphs 2 and 3 do the SAME job (both give supporting examples for paragraph 1's claim). How many boxes should your map have — 6, or fewer? Why?
SHSAT Trap: One Box Per Paragraph Isn't the Rule
Structure questions test the passage's LOGIC, not its paragraph count. Multiple paragraphs doing the same job collapse into one box; one paragraph doing two jobs can split into two boxes.
Zone 1 · Practice, Set 2

Naming the Box — Harder Structure Calls

5Level 3
A passage's map is: Myth → Evidence Against Myth → Explanation of Why Myth Persists → Call to Action. A question asks why the author includes a paragraph about why people still believe the myth. Which box answers this, and what's the box's job in one phrase?
6Level 3
A fiction passage's map is: Setting → Conflict Introduced → Rising Tension → Turning Point → Resolution. Which two boxes would a question about "the moment the story's outcome becomes clear" most likely target?
7Level 4
A passage compares two scientific theories throughout, going back and forth: Theory A → Theory B → Theory A again → Theory B again → Synthesis. Is this passage organized by TIME, by CONTRAST, or by something else — and how would you draw that on a map that isn't a straight line?
8Level 4
Two students map the SAME passage differently — one has 4 boxes, one has 6. Both get every structure question right. What does this tell you about whether there's one "correct" map?
SHSAT Trap: Confusing "What Happens" with "What Job It Does"
A box label like "the fire happens" describes an event, not a job. A better label is "conflict escalates" — it tells you WHY that paragraph exists in the passage's argument or story.
Zone 2 · Area & Perimeter

Perimeter Is the Fence; Area Is the Lawn

Think about it
If you walk around the OUTSIDE edge of a rectangular yard, are you measuring perimeter or area? If you're covering the INSIDE with sod, which one is that? Now: does a circle have a "perimeter," or does it get a special name?
Build it yourself — don't just memorize it
Rectangle: P=2l+2w, A=lw  ·  Triangle: A=½bh  ·  Circle: C=2πr, A=πr²
Why this is the clever way
"Perimeter" and "circumference" are both just "distance around the outside" — one word for straight-sided shapes, one for circles. If you remember that ONE idea, you never have to guess which formula measures what.
Zone 2 · Practice, Set 1

Area & Perimeter — Easy to Hard

9Level 1
A rectangle has length 9 and width 4. Write the formula first, then find the perimeter AND the area.
10Level 1
A triangle has base 10 and height 6. Write the area formula, then compute. Why does the formula have a ½ in it — what shape would you get WITHOUT the ½?
11Level 2
A circle has radius 7. Find its circumference and its area (leave answers in terms of π). Which formula uses r once, and which one uses it squared — and why does that make sense given what area measures?
12Level 2
A rectangular garden has a perimeter of 36 feet and a length of 12 feet. Set up the perimeter formula and solve for the width first — then find the area.
SHSAT Trap: Diameter vs. Radius
Circle problems love to give you the diameter and hope you plug it straight into a formula that needs the radius. Always write "r = d ÷ 2" as your very first step when a diameter appears.
Zone 2 · Practice, Set 2

Composite Shapes — Break It Into Pieces

13Level 3
A figure is a 12-by-8 rectangle with a right triangle (base 6, height 8) attached to one side, as shown. What's the total area? Which piece do you compute first, and does it matter?
14Level 3
A square lawn (side 10) has a circular fountain (radius 2) cut out of the middle. What is the remaining lawn area? What operation connects the two shapes — adding or subtracting?
15Level 4
A running track is a rectangle (length 40, width 20) with a semicircle (radius 10) attached to each short end. What's the total perimeter of the track? Do the two semicircles combine into one full circle's circumference, or two separate arcs?
SHSAT Trap: Cut-Out Shapes Subtract, Attached Shapes Add
A fountain removed FROM a lawn subtracts area. A triangle attached TO a rectangle adds area. Always ask "is this piece INSIDE (remove it) or ADDED ON (include it)?" before combining formulas.
Zone 3 · Coordinate Plane

Distance on a Grid Is Just a Hidden Right Triangle

Think about it
Two points share the same row or same column on a grid. Can you find the distance between them just by counting boxes? Now two points DON'T share a row or column — what shape do you get if you connect them with a horizontal leg and a vertical leg?
Two grid tools, both from counting
Same row/column: count boxes  ·  Diagonal: count legs, then Pythagorean theorem
Why this is the clever way
The SHSAT's coordinate questions rarely need the full distance formula memorized — most give you nice grid points where you can literally count the horizontal and vertical legs, then just apply a²+b²=c².
Zone 3 · Practice

Coordinate Plane — Easy to Hard

16Level 1
Point A is at (2, 3) and point B is at (2, 9). What's the distance between them? Do you need the Pythagorean theorem here — why or why not?
17Level 1
Point C is at (1, 4) and point D is at (7, 4). Find the midpoint by counting boxes along the row — what point sits exactly between them?
18Level 2
Point E is at (1, 1) and point F is at (4, 5). Count the horizontal leg and the vertical leg first, then use a²+b²=c² to find the distance.
19Level 2
Point G is at (−3, 2) and point H is at (1, 2). What's the distance? What does a negative x-coordinate change about how you count boxes?
20Level 3
A square is plotted with two of its corners at (0, 0) and (0, 6). If the square's area is 36, where could the other two corners be? Sketch it — what does the side length have to be?
21Level 4
Point J is the midpoint of segment KL. K is at (2, 3) and J is at (5, 8). Where is point L? What operation "undoes" a midpoint calculation?
SHSAT Trap: Mixing Up Which Coordinate Changes
When points share the same y-coordinate, the DISTANCE lives in the x-coordinates (and vice versa). Circle the coordinate that's identical first — that tells you which numbers to subtract.
Geometry · Wrap-Up Check

Name the Tool Before You Reach for a Formula

Think about it
The SHSAT often mixes area, perimeter, and coordinate questions in the same set. Before writing anything, ask: "is this asking about the OUTSIDE of a shape, the INSIDE of a shape, or the DISTANCE between two points on a grid?"
SkillPerimeter
The outside
Add up every side, or use 2πr for circles. Units are always linear (feet, cm — never squared).
SkillArea
The inside
Multiply the two dimensions that define the flat space. Units are always squared.
SkillCoordinate
Count first, then compute
Same row/column: just count. Diagonal: count both legs, then Pythagorean theorem.
The self-made formula sheet
Before next week, write out all six formulas from today (rectangle P & A, triangle A, circle C & A, distance/midpoint) on one card in your own handwriting. Writing it yourself is what makes it stick — a printed sheet doesn't.
Zone 4 · Timed Editing Warm-Up3x/Week Habit Starts

10 Minutes, One Rule at a Time

Think about it
"After the game, the team, celebrated at the diner." Is there a comma error here — and if so, is the problem an EXTRA comma or a MISSING one? What's the rule about commas after introductory phrases versus commas splitting a subject from its verb?
Today's two rules on watch
1) Introductory phrases get ONE comma after them, not a comma splitting subject from verb. 2) Two complete sentences joined without a conjunction need a semicolon, comma+conjunction, or period — never just a comma alone.
Why this is the clever way
Writing/Editing dropped from 90 percent to 40 percent — likely rust, not a knowledge gap. Naming ONE specific rule per error (not just "that sounds wrong") is what turns a guess into a skill that survives under time pressure.
SHSAT Trap: "It Sounds Right" Isn't a Rule
If you can't name the specific comma or sentence rule being tested, you're guessing by ear — which is exactly how a strong 90 percent score can slip. Name the rule, every time.
