System 1 · Ratios

A Ratio Is a Comparison With a Direction

Read before reducing
“Red to blue is 3:5” compares two parts. “Red out of all tiles” changes the question: the whole has 3 + 5 equal ratio-parts.
part : part → part / (part + part)
Ask before every ratio problem
Am I comparing part to part, part to whole, or one quantity per one unit of another quantity?
Worked Model 1 · Three Moves

Convert Part-to-Part Without Losing the Whole

Model: In a club, the ratio of sixth graders to seventh graders is 4:7. What percent of the club is in seventh grade?
The tempting wrong denominator
Seven is one part, not the whole. A percent needs “out of all,” so build the whole first.
1

Name the relationship

4:7 is part-to-part. Label the parts before touching the numbers.

2

Build the whole

Total ratio-parts = 4 + 7 = 11. Seventh graders occupy 7 of those 11 parts.

3

Translate to percent

Use 7/11 × 100%. Estimate first: it is more than one-half but less than two-thirds.

Clever check
The two group percents must total 100%. If your seventh-grade percent plus your sixth-grade percent does not, the whole was mishandled.
System 2 · Proportions

Scale the Table — Do Not Cross-Multiply Blindly

What stayed constant?
The recipe did not change. Each row is the same relationship at a different size, so every quantity must be multiplied by the same scale factor.
new = original × scale factor
When a proportion is not allowed
If there is a fixed starting fee, an initial amount, or a changing rate, the relationship may not pass through the origin.
System 3 · Unit Rates

Let the Units Choose the Division

Read “per” as a fraction bar
Miles per hour means miles divided by hours. Dollars per ounce means dollars divided by ounces. Put the requested unit on top.
requested unit / 1 comparison unit
Before dividing, predict
If 180 miles happen over 3 hours, should one hour contain fewer than 180 miles or more? The story gives the direction of the answer.
Worked Model 2 · Three Moves

Use a Scale Factor That Both Columns Obey

Model: On a map, 1.5 inches represents 24 miles. Two towns are 4.25 inches apart. Find the actual distance.
1

Write matched units

24 miles / 1.5 inches. Keep miles above inches in both ratios.

2

Find the unit rate

Scale both numbers to “per 1 inch” before applying the longer map distance.

3

Scale to 4.25 inches

Multiply the miles-per-inch rate by 4.25 inches; inches cancel, leaving miles.

Clever check
4.25 inches is a little less than three times 1.5 inches, so the real distance must be a little less than three times 24 miles.
System 4 · Percent

Percent Is a Ratio Whose Whole Is 100

Three translations
p% of W: multiply.
P is what percent of W: divide part by whole.
Increase/decrease: compare the change to the original.
Hidden-base trap
In “40 is 25% more than what number?” 40 is the new amount, not the original base.
Percent Multipliers

Keep the Percent Change Attached to Its Base

Percent of
new = p × original
Use the decimal form of the percent.
Increase
new = (1 + p) × original
Keep the original 100%, then add p.
Decrease
new = (1 − p) × original
Keep what remains after the reduction.
Why multipliers are safer
A 30% discount means you keep 70%, so multiplying by 0.70 captures the subtraction in one visible move. Tax or markup comes afterward and uses the amount that exists at that moment.
Worked Model 3 · Repeated Change

Up 20%, Then Down 20% Does Not Return Home

1

Represent the increase

Multiply the original by 1.20. The new amount becomes the next base.

2

Represent the decrease

Multiply that new amount by 0.80, not the original amount.

3

Compare with the start

Combine the multipliers: 1.20 × 0.80. Compare the product with 1.

Equal percents, unequal amounts
The decrease acts on a larger base than the increase did. Percent changes cancel only when the actual added and subtracted amounts match.
System 5 · Relationship Test

Proportional Means Constant Rate + Origin

Three quick tests
Table: is y/x constant?
Equation: is it y = kx with no added constant?
Graph: is it a straight line through the origin?
Distinguish “linear” from “proportional”
A straight line can still be non-proportional. A taxi fare with a $4 starting charge is linear, but zero miles does not cost zero dollars.
