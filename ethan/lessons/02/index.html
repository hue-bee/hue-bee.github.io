Launch · Retrieval

What Does Change Look Like?

ELA lens
A passage begins by praising a new invention, then spends two paragraphs warning about its costs. Where does the author's route turn? What would you call the paragraph that creates that turn?
Math lens
A tank loses 18 liters during 6 minutes. Which quantity is the input? Which is the output? What does “per minute” force into the denominator?
Today's shared habit
Do not hunt for a label or formula first. Track the movement: idea → idea in ELA; input → output in math. The direction of change is the clue.
ELA Tool 1 · Passage Blueprint

Paragraphs Are Moves, Not Containers

INTRO
What problem, debate, person, or question enters the room?
SUPPORT
What evidence, example, mechanism, or explanation builds the case?
PIVOT
Where does direction change: however, limitation, surprise, competing view?
CONCLUSION
What larger meaning, implication, judgment, or next step closes the route?
Ask function, not topic
“This paragraph is about trees” names content. “This paragraph complicates the proposed tree-planting solution” names its job. Which description helps more on an organization question—and why?
Clever test: remove it mentally
If the passage loses its setup, it was an intro. If proof disappears, support. If the argument no longer turns, pivot. If the “so what?” vanishes, conclusion.

Passage Set A · Read & Map

Cooling a City, One Roof at a Time

Original nonfiction passage · paragraphs 1–2 of 4

1On summer afternoons, a city block can remain several degrees warmer than nearby parkland. Pavement and dark roofs absorb sunlight during the day and release that stored heat after sunset, when residents expect relief. To counter this “urban heat island” effect, several cities have encouraged building owners to coat rooftops with pale, reflective material. The proposal sounds almost mechanical: send more sunlight back toward the sky, and the neighborhood should cool.

2Early trials appeared to justify that confidence. Sensors placed above treated roofs recorded lower surface temperatures than sensors above untreated roofs. Indoor temperatures sometimes fell as well, reducing the need for air-conditioning during peak hours. Because one coated roof is inexpensive compared with rebuilding a street or planting mature trees, officials began to describe reflective roofing as a fast, scalable response to dangerous heat.

Paragraph 1
What enters the room: a solution, or the problem that makes a solution necessary? Tag the function; then write exactly five words.
Paragraph 2
Is the author changing direction yet, or strengthening the proposal? What kind of evidence does the paragraph contribute?
Passage Set A · Continue

Watch for the Turn

Paragraphs 3–4 of 4

3Yet a cooler roof does not automatically produce a cooler neighborhood. Reflected energy may strike taller buildings, and the glare can make nearby sidewalks uncomfortable. More importantly, roof coatings do little for pedestrians on blocks with scarce shade. A person waiting for a bus experiences heat radiating from pavement and passing vehicles, not merely from the building overhead. The intervention's success therefore depends on what is being measured—and where.

4Reflective roofs remain useful, but the evidence argues against treating them as a complete remedy. Cities can pair them with shade trees, lighter paving materials, and cooling centers, selecting combinations that fit each block. The broader lesson is not that simple tools fail; it is that a city is a system, so a promising local improvement must be tested against conditions beyond the surface it changes.

Find the hinge
Which word announces that the passage will complicate its earlier confidence? If paragraph 3 vanished, what mistaken impression would remain?
Find the “so what?”
Does paragraph 4 reject reflective roofs, narrow the claim, or broaden the lesson? Capture its function in five words.

ELA Strategy · Organization Traps

Topic Is What; Function Is Why Here?

Trap 1 · Accurate but useless
“Paragraph 3 discusses pedestrians” is true, but it does not explain why pedestrians appear after promising roof data.
Trap 2 · Overstating the turn
A pivot may qualify an idea without destroying it. Words such as limits, complicates, narrows are often more precise than refutes.
Trap 3 · Ignoring neighbors
A paragraph's role depends on what comes before and after it. Test the bridge on both sides.
Fast verbal stem
“The author includes this paragraph after ___ in order to ___, which prepares the reader for ___.” Fill all three blanks before committing.
Passage Set B · Transfer

When Quiet Walls Refuse to Be Smooth

Original nonfiction passage · 4 short paragraphs

1Designers often try to block city noise with tall, solid barriers. Such walls can stop a direct path between a roadway and nearby homes, but flat surfaces also reflect sound, redirecting some energy toward buildings across the street.

2Researchers studying owl wings noticed a different possibility. The uneven leading edges of certain feathers break moving air into smaller currents, reducing the sharp pressure changes that create audible sound. Engineers wondered whether an irregular wall could similarly scatter sound waves rather than merely bounce them elsewhere.

3Small models supported the analogy, but full streets introduced complications. Grooves that scattered one frequency sometimes reflected another, while rainwater and debris altered the wall's surface. A design successful in a controlled chamber could therefore perform unpredictably beside traffic.

4The setback changed the research question. Instead of seeking one ideal pattern, engineers began testing adjustable panels whose angles could be tuned for local traffic and weather. Biology supplied the opening idea; the city forced that idea to become flexible.

Math Transition · Rate Thinking

Slope Answers One Question: How Much per One?

Build the fraction with words
If water depth changes as time passes, which belongs on top: change in depth or change in time? Read the unit “centimeters per minute” aloud. Which word marks the denominator?
Output change per input change
slope = Δy / Δx = (y₂ − y₁) / (x₂ − x₁)
Clever explanation: slope is an exchange rate
It tells what the graph “pays out” in y for each one-unit step in x. A negative rate means the output is being taken away, not that the calculation failed.
Math Tool · Three Representations

Different Pictures, Same Rate

TABLE · compare steps
x−214
y115−1
Ask: when x changes by 3, how does y change? Then reduce to a one-unit x step.
GRAPH · draw the rate triangle
Count using axis values, not squares. A graph may skip by 2, 5, or 10.
TWO POINTS · preserve order
(−4, 9) and (2, −3)
Pick a direction. Subtract y-values in that direction, then x-values in the same direction. Switching only one order flips the sign.
Invariant check
If all three represent the same line, what must stay identical: the coordinates, the visual steepness, or the output change per one input? How could you verify without finishing all arithmetic?
Math Strategy · Units & Meaning

A Number Is Not a Rate Until It Has Units

Sentence frame
“For every additional 1 [x-unit], the [y-quantity] increases/decreases by ___ [y-units].”
Intercept trap
Slope describes change. The y-intercept describes the starting output when x = 0. A large starting value does not mean a steep rate.
Scale trap
Rise 4 squares and run 2 squares is not automatically slope 2. First translate each square into axis units.
