Map the project: problem → testing → revision → result → next step. For each question, identify the exact word your evidence must prove.

Cooling the Courtyard

1 At Harbor Middle School, a broad asphalt courtyard offered the only outdoor seating near the cafeteria. By late spring, however, most students crowded along one narrow wall where the building cast a strip of shade. The science club suspected that heat—not a lack of interest—was keeping students away from the benches. Over six cloudless days, members measured surface temperatures at the same three times each day. The open blacktop averaged 14°F warmer than the shaded sidewalk.

2 The club first considered planting mature trees, but large trees would exceed the available budget, while young ones would take years to provide useful shade. Members then investigated two faster approaches. Reflective pavement coating cooled the ground, yet their tests showed little change in air temperature at seated height. Fabric shade sails blocked direct sunlight immediately, but they required anchors strong enough to withstand wind.

3 Rather than select the least expensive sail, the students built scale models and used a fan to compare the force on different shapes. Their first design—a single triangular sheet pulled tightly between three posts—cast a wide shadow. During an outdoor trial, though, a gust loosened one anchor. The students replaced the large sheet with smaller overlapping panels. Narrow gaps between the panels allowed air to pass through, reducing force while preserving nearly the same noon shadow.

4 The principal approved a four-week pilot over one seating area. Cafeteria staff counted students who chose outdoor seats and compared the totals with records from the same weeks of the previous year. On mild days, use of the outdoor seats nearly doubled. On the hottest afternoons, however, it rose only slightly.

5 The club concluded that shade improved the courtyard but could not solve the heat problem by itself. Its final report recommended adding a water-bottle filling station and training vines along trellises beside the seating area. The report clearly separated results the pilot had demonstrated from improvements the students still needed to test.

6 The district used the report to fund a second set of shade panels and a row of drought-tolerant vines. For the students, the most valuable outcome was not a single structure. Their measurements had turned a familiar complaint into a design problem that could be tested, revised, and tested again.
