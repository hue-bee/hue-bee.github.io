Part A · Math Core

Problems 1–7

Tutor habit for this set: translate before you calculate. For fractions, make the units match; for decimals, estimate first; for complex fractions, simplify each layer before you flip anything.

Problem 1 L2

Find 2/3 + 3/4.

Strategy: Different denominators mean the pieces are different sizes, so you must rename them before adding. Least common denominator of 3 and 4 is 12. 2/3 = 8/12 and 3/4 = 9/12. 8/12 + 9/12 = 17/12 = 1 5/12.
Answer: 1 5/12
SHSAT Trap: Students often add across and write 5/7. That only works when denominators already match.
Problem 2 L4

A recipe needs 2 1/3 cups of flour, but you are making 3/4 of the recipe. How much flour do you need?

Strategy: “Of” means multiply. Convert the mixed number before multiplying, or the setup breaks. 2 1/3 = 7/3. 7/3 × 3/4 = 21/12. Simplify: 21/12 = 7/4 = 1 3/4 cups.
Answer: 1 3/4 cups
SHSAT Trap: Do not multiply 2 by 3/4 and then tack on 1/3. Mixed numbers must become improper fractions first.
Problem 3 L2

Find 3.6 × 0.4.

Strategy: Estimate before computing. Since 0.4 is less than 1, the answer must be smaller than 3.6. Ignore decimals first: 36 × 4 = 144. There are 2 total decimal places, so place the point two spots from the right. 144 → 1.44.
Answer: 1.44
Quick check: If you got 14.4, your decimal point drifted. The estimate catches that immediately.
Problem 4 L4

A stack of 12 identical textbooks is 28.8 cm tall. What is the thickness of one textbook?

Strategy: Total ÷ number of books = one book. This is unit rate thinking. 28.8 ÷ 12. 12 goes into 28 two times (24), remainder 4.8. 4.8 ÷ 12 = 0.4, so 2 + 0.4 = 2.4.
Answer: 2.4 cm
SHSAT Trap: Some students divide 12 by 28.8, which reverses the unit rate and answers the wrong question.
Problem 5 L3

Order from least to greatest: 5/8, 0.6, 7/10, 0.58.

Strategy: One common form settles every argument. Convert everything to decimals. 5/8 = 0.625, 0.6 = 0.600, 7/10 = 0.700, and 0.58 stays 0.580. Compare place value digit by digit: 0.580 < 0.600 < 0.625 < 0.700.
Answer: 0.58, 0.6, 5/8, 7/10
SHSAT Trap: Eyeballing 5/8 as “5 is less than 7” misses the fact that the denominators change the size of each part.
Problem 6 L3

Simplify (3/4) ÷ (5/8).

Strategy: A fraction bar means division, so use keep-change-flip. 3/4 ÷ 5/8 = 3/4 × 8/5. Multiply: 3×8 = 24 and 4×5 = 20. 24/20 simplifies to 6/5 = 1 1/5.
Answer: 1 1/5
Smart move: You could also cross-reduce first: 8 ÷ 4 = 2, so the multiplication becomes 3×2/5 = 6/5.
Problem 7 L4

Simplify (1 1/2) ÷ (1/4 + 2/3). Combine the bottom before you flip anything.

Strategy: This problem warns you about the trap directly: simplify the denominator first because it has more than one term. Convert the mixed number: 1 1/2 = 3/2. Add the denominator: 1/4 + 2/3 = 3/12 + 8/12 = 11/12. Now divide: 3/2 ÷ 11/12 = 3/2 × 12/11 = 36/22 = 18/11 = 1 7/11.
Answer: 1 7/11
SHSAT Trap: Flipping too early creates nonsense because you would be inverting only part of the denominator instead of the entire quantity 11/12.

Part B · ELA Modifiers

Problems 8–9

Fastest modifier test: ask, “Who is doing the action in the opening phrase?” If the subject right after the comma cannot logically do that action, the sentence is dangling.

Q8. Which sentence fixes the dangling modifier? Covered in frosting, Maria cut the cake. L2
Why B: The opening phrase Covered in frosting should describe the cake, not Maria. Choice B puts the cake immediately after the modifier, so the sentence becomes logical and grammatically clean.
Why not A? A is logical and natural in everyday writing, but it changes the structure instead of directly repairing the modifier pattern the exercise is targeting. B is the clean textbook fix because the modified noun appears right after the phrase.
Q9. Identify the misplaced modifier: She almost finished all of her homework before dinner. L3
Why A: As written, almost finished all of her homework suggests she did not quite finish. If the intended meaning is that she finished nearly all of it, the modifier belongs next to all, as in She finished almost all of her homework before dinner.
Test-day strategy: Read the sentence two ways. If moving one small word changes the meaning, you found the misplaced modifier.

Tutor Notes · High-Value Habits

  • Fractions: Rename before adding or subtracting, and simplify at the end—not halfway unless you are cross-reducing in multiplication or division.
  • Decimals: Estimate first so the decimal point has to earn its spot.
  • Complex fractions: If the top or bottom has more than one term, simplify that group completely before using keep-change-flip.
  • Modifiers: After an opening phrase, the next noun must be the thing doing or receiving that action logically.
