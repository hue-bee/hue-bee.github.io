Zone 5 · Practice

Three Ways to Check Your Work

Worked example: 3.6 × 0.4 = ?
✓Before You Start
Estimate First
Round to 4 × 0.4 = 1.6. So the real answer should land close to 1.6 — definitely under 3.6. If you calculate and get something like 14.4, that's a decimal-point red flag before you even check the arithmetic.
✓After You Finish
Plug It Back In
You calculated 3.6 × 0.4 = 1.44. Does 1.44 actually satisfy "0.4 of 3.6"? Divide 1.44 ÷ 0.4 — if you get back 3.6, your answer checks out.
✓If Time Allows
Redo It a Different Way
Rewrite as fractions: 36/10 × 4/10 = 144/100 = 1.44. Same answer from a totally different path — that's strong proof 1.44 is correct.
⚠️ SHSAT Trap: Confidence Without Proof
Feeling sure isn't the same as being right. Before you bubble in an answer, ask yourself which check above you actually used on 3.6 × 0.4 — not just whether 1.44 "looks fine."
Zone 1 · Fraction Operations

Common Denominators Are the Universal Translator

Think about it
Can you add thirds and fourths directly, the way you'd add 3 apples and 4 apples? Think it through — the pieces are different sizes, so you can't just combine them yet.
a/b + c/d = (a·d + b·c) / (b·d)  →  simplify last
Zone 1 · Practice

Fractions — Easy to Hard

1Level 2
Find 2/3 + 3/4. Ask yourself first: what's the smallest number both 3 and 4 divide into?
2Level 4
A recipe needs 2 1/3 cups of flour, but you're making 3/4 of the recipe. How much flour do you need?
⚠️ SHSAT Trap: Mixed Numbers into Multiplication
A common mistake is multiplying the whole number and fraction parts separately. Always convert a mixed number to an improper fraction before you multiply or divide.
Zone 2 · Decimal Operations

Place Value Never Lies

Think about it
When you multiply 1.2 × 0.5, roughly how big should the answer be — bigger or smaller than 1.2? Estimate before you calculate so you can sanity-check your own work.
Memory shortcut
  • Multiply as whole numbers first, ignore the decimal point.
  • Count total decimal places in both factors.
  • Move the point that many places in the final answer.
  • Dividing by a decimal: shift both points until the divisor is a whole number.
Zone 2 · Practice

Decimals — Easy to Hard

3Level 2
Find 3.6 × 0.4. Estimate first — should the answer be bigger or smaller than 3.6?
4Level 4
A stack of 12 identical textbooks is 28.8 cm tall. What is the thickness of one textbook, in centimeters?
⚠️ SHSAT Trap: Miscounted Decimal Places
The single most common decimal error is placing the point in the wrong spot after multiplying. Count the digits after the point in each factor, then add those counts together — every time.
Zone 3 · Comparing & Converting

One Common Form Settles Every Argument

🤔 Think about it
Which is bigger: 5/8 or 0.62? How could you find out for sure, without guessing? Try converting the fraction to a decimal (or the decimal to a fraction) instead of eyeballing it.
The rule
To compare a fraction and a decimal, convert both to the same format. Divide the fraction's numerator by its denominator to get a decimal — or write the decimal as a fraction over a power of 10.
5Level 3
Order from least to greatest: 5/8, 0.6, 7/10, 0.58
Zone 4 · Complex Fractions — Stretch Goal

A Fraction Bar Just Means "Divided By"

🤔 Think about it
If a/b just means a ÷ b, what does a fraction sitting on top of another fraction actually mean? Try to say it in your own words before you look at the shortcut below.
Keep — Change — Flip
Keep the first fraction, change ÷ to ×, flip the second fraction upside down. A complex fraction is nothing but hidden division.
(a/b) / (c/d) = a/b × d/c
Zone 4 · Practice

Complex Fractions — Easy to Hard

6Level 3
Simplify: (3/4) / (5/8)
7Level 4
Simplify: (1 1/2 + 1/4) / (2/3)  — combine the top before you flip anything.
⚠️ SHSAT Trap: Flipping Too Early
If the numerator or denominator has more than one term, simplify that part completely first. Flipping before combining is the single most common complex-fraction mistake — don't fall for it.

ELA Zone · Modifiers

If It Can't Logically Modify the Subject, Move It

🤔 Think about it
"Running down the street, the ice cream melted in the sun." Who's running — the ice cream, or a person? Spot the absurdity first, then we'll name the rule: dangling modifier.
Two modifier errors
  • Dangling: the modifier has nothing logical to describe.
  • Misplaced: the modifier sits next to the wrong noun.
  • Fix: place the modifying phrase directly next to the noun it should describe.
