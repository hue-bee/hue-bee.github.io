Part B — Signed Numbers & Absolute Value

Problems 1–8

Every solution below applies the same three-step check: rewrite double signs, decide the final sign before computing, and confirm by plugging the answer back in.

Problem 1 L1

Add the two signed numbers.

−9 + (−6) = ?
Same signs (both negative) → add the values and keep the negative sign. 9 + 6 = 15, so the result is −15.
Answer: −15
Problem 2 L1

Find the value using absolute value.

|−12| − 5 = ?
|−12| strips the sign → 12. 12 − 5 = 7.
Answer: 7
Problem 3 L2

Multiply the two negatives.

−4 × (−7) = ?
Two negatives (even count) → the product is positive. 4 × 7 = 28.
Answer: 28
Problem 4 L2

Divide, then add.

15 ÷ (−3) + (−2) = ?
Division follows the same sign rule as multiplication: positive ÷ negative → negative. 15 ÷ (−3) = −5. −5 + (−2) = −7 (same signs → add, keep negative).
Answer: −7
Problem 5 L2

Rewrite the double sign before you do anything else.

8 − (−5) − 12 = ?
8 − (−5) − 12 → 8 + 5 − 12 (subtracting a negative = adding a positive). 8 + 5 = 13, then 13 − 12 = 1.
Answer: 1
Problem 6 L3

Multiply three negatives together.

−3 × (−2) × (−5) = ?
Three negatives (odd count) → the product is negative, regardless of grouping order. 3 × 2 × 5 = 30, so the result is −30.
Answer: −30
Problem 7 L3

Simplify fully inside each absolute value bar before subtracting.

|−6 + 2| − |4 − 9| = ?
|−6 + 2| = |−4| = 4. |4 − 9| = |−5| = 5. 4 − 5 = −1.
Answer: −1
⚠️ SHSAT Trap: Absolute value bars are grouping symbols — each bar must be fully simplified on its own before the outside subtraction happens. Combining the insides first (e.g., treating it like |(−6+2)−(4−9)|) gives a wrong answer.
Problem 8 L3

Follow order of operations, then rewrite the double sign.

−8 − (−15) + (−6) × 2 = ?
Multiply first: (−6) × 2 = −12. −8 − (−15) + (−12) → −8 + 15 − 12 (rewrite the double sign). −8 + 15 = 7, then 7 − 12 = −5.
Answer: −5

Part C — Multi-Step, Test-Day Format

Problem 9 · Level 4

Translate the words into a signed expression first, then compute — this is exactly how the answer choices are designed to catch translation errors.

Problem 9 L4

A submarine is at an elevation of −120 feet. It rises 45 feet, then descends 3 times that distance. What is its final elevation?

Start: −120 feet. "Rises 45 feet" → add 45: −120 + 45 = −75. "Descends 3 times that distance" → 3 × 45 = 135, and descending means subtract: −75 − 135 = −210.
Answer: C — −210 feet
Why the traps are wrong: Choice A (−75) stops after the rise and forgets the descent entirely. Choice B (−195) uses the wrong descent multiplier instead of 3 × 45 = 135. Choice D (165 feet) drops the sign convention for elevation and treats "descend" as addition instead of subtraction.

Part D — ELA: Pronoun Clarity & Agreement

Questions 1–4

The fastest check: point to the exact word each pronoun replaces. If you can't point to exactly one word, the sentence has a problem.

Q1. Which is correct? "Neither of the coaches submitted ___ roster on time." L2
Why B: "Neither" is grammatically singular on the SHSAT, so its pronoun must also be singular. "His" (used generically here) matches "neither," while "their" incorrectly treats "neither" as plural, and "they're"/"our" don't fit the sentence's subject at all.
Q2. Which sentence fixes the unclear pronoun reference? "Maria told her sister that she had won the scholarship." L3
Why A: The original sentence's "she" could point to either Maria or her sister. Choice A removes the ambiguity by naming "Maria" directly instead of using a pronoun. Choices B, C, and D keep an ambiguous "she" or reorder the sentence without ever clarifying who won.
Q3. "Each of the students must bring ___ own permission slip." Which pronoun agrees correctly? L2
Why B: "Each" is singular on the SHSAT (just like "neither," "everyone," and "every"), so it needs a singular pronoun. "His or her" is the grammatically correct singular match; "their" and "they" incorrectly pluralize the subject, and "our" doesn't agree with "each" at all.
Q4. "When Jenna met Amara, she gave her the report." Why is this sentence ambiguous, and how would you fix it? L4
Why A: With two female names in the sentence, both "she" and "her" have two equally valid antecedents, so the reader can't tell who gave the report to whom. The fix is always the same on the SHSAT: swap one pronoun for the actual name (e.g., "When Jenna met Amara, Jenna gave her the report").
🤔 Tutor shortcut for all four: Teach students to ask one question every time — "Is the noun singular or plural in the SHSAT's eyes, and can I point to exactly one word this pronoun replaces?" Q1 and Q3 test singular indefinite pronouns; Q2 and Q4 test ambiguous antecedents with two equally valid nouns.

🎯 Tutor Notes — Common Error Patterns to Watch For

  • Concept gap: Student doesn't know the sign rule (e.g., thinks two negatives multiplied stay negative) — reteach with the even/odd negatives rule.
  • Careless error: Student knows the rule but drops a sign when rewriting double signs or forgets to multiply before adding (Order of Operations) — reinforce the "rewrite double sign first" habit.
  • Rushed/skipped step: Student jumps straight to an answer on Problem 7 or 9 without simplifying each absolute value bar or translating the word problem into numbers first — slow down and require the work box to be filled in fully.
  • Singular/plural confusion (ELA): Words like "neither," "each," and "everyone" are singular on the SHSAT even though they feel plural — this is the most common trap in Q1 and Q3-style questions.
  • Ambiguous antecedent (ELA): When two nouns of the same gender/number appear before a pronoun, students often miss that the pronoun could logically point to either one — train students to "point to the word" as a fast self-check on Q2 and Q4-style questions.
