Part D — Reading Evidence Lab

7 Questions · ~22 min

Map each paragraph’s job in two or three words. Then return to the smallest proof window that answers the question.

The Archive Inside a Tree

1 To a passerby, the old pine at the edge of a mountain meadow may seem motionless. To a scientist, however, its trunk is a record that has been writing itself for centuries. Each year the tree adds a thin layer of wood beneath its bark. The visible pattern of those layers, known as rings, can preserve evidence about the conditions under which the tree grew.

2 A wide ring often signals a favorable growing season, while a narrow ring may indicate drought, unusual cold, insect damage, or competition from neighboring trees. Yet one tree alone rarely settles the matter. A narrow ring could have several causes, and an injury might distort growth on only one side of the trunk. Researchers therefore compare many trees from the same region before drawing conclusions.

3 The comparison begins by matching distinctive sequences. Suppose several living trees display two narrow rings followed by one unusually wide ring. Wood from an older fallen tree may contain that same sequence near its outer edge. By aligning the shared pattern, researchers can extend the timeline backward beyond the age of any single living tree. This process is called cross-dating.

4 Cross-dated records can help scholars investigate more than rainfall. In some regions, buildings were made with local timber. Matching the rings in a roof beam to a regional timeline can reveal when the tree was cut and can narrow the possible construction date. Similar comparisons have helped researchers study wildfire frequency, glacier movement, and changes in river flow.

5 Tree rings do not provide a complete diary. They record growth, not a written explanation of every event. Their value comes from careful comparison with other evidence, such as weather records, charcoal layers, and historical documents. A ring is best understood not as a final answer but as a dated clue—one that becomes more useful when placed beside other clues.
