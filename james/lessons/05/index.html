Sprint · Substitution Warm-Up

Eight Quick Ones: Beat Your Own Score

Think first
If y = 4x and you need y + 3 in terms of x, what's the very first move — solve for something, or replace y right where it sits?
Why this matters
Substitution is the engine behind every multi-relationship word problem today. If swapping a variable for its equivalent expression is automatic, the harder word problems later become pure setup, not new math.
Sprint items — 8, timed, mental math
y=4x, find y+3  ·  a=2b, find 3a−b  ·  m=n+5, find 2m  ·  y=3x, find y−x
Your Habit Target
Say the substitution out loud before writing it: "y is standing in for 4x, so everywhere I see y, I write 4x instead." Naming the swap out loud is what makes it stick as a motor skill, not just a fact you know.
Zone 1 · SubstitutionSubstitute First

One Variable Is Just Another One in Disguise

Think about it
If y = 3x, is y a totally separate quantity from x — or is it just a nickname for "3x"? If it's a nickname, what happens the instant you replace every "y" with what it's a nickname for?
The rule, every time
Substitute first. Simplify second. Never the reverse.
Why this is the clever way
Two letters in one expression means two things to track at once — that's where sign errors and dropped terms happen. The instant you replace y with 3x, the whole problem collapses down to ONE variable. From there it's just ordinary distribute-and-combine, the exact same motion James already does automatically.
Zone 1 · Deep Dive

Worked Step-by-Step: 2y + 4(2y + 3) − x

1
Identify the substitution rule. We're told y = 3x. Every "y" in the expression is a stand-in for "3x."
2
Replace every y, don't skip any. 2y + 4(2y + 3) − x becomes 2(3x) + 4(2(3x) + 3) − x. Both y's get swapped — the one alone and the one inside the parentheses.
3
Simplify inside the parentheses first. 2(3x) = 6x, so inside becomes 6x + 3. Expression is now 6x + 4(6x + 3) − x.
4
Distribute the 4 across both terms. 4(6x + 3) = 24x + 12. Now: 6x + 24x + 12 − x.
5
Combine all the x-terms. 6x + 24x − x = 29x... wait — recount: 6x + 24x = 30x, then 30x − x = 29x. Combine constants separately: just +12.
Final answer
29x + 12. Notice the process never required tracking y and x at the same time after Step 2 — that's the entire point of substituting first.
Zone 1 · Practice

Substitution — Easy to Hard

1Level 1
If a = 5b, what is a + 2b in terms of b? Swap the a first — what's left to combine?
2Level 1
If m = n − 4, what is 3m + n in terms of n? Replace m everywhere it appears, then combine like terms.
3Level 2
If p = 2q, simplify 3(p + q) − q. Substitute before you open the parentheses — does that save a step?
4Level 3
If y = 3x, simplify 2y + 4(2y + 3) − x completely — today's deep-dive example. Rebuild the five steps from memory without peeking back.
5Level 4
If r = 2s + 1, simplify 5(r − s) + 3(s − r) in terms of s. Two substitutions of the same expression, then combine — does distributing negative 3 change the sign of every term inside?
6Level 4
If k = 4h − 2, simplify 2(k + 3h) − (k − h) in terms of h. Substitute k in both places it appears before distributing anything — do the two k's simplify the same way?
SHSAT Trap: Simplifying Before Substituting
Distributing or combining terms while two different letters are still mixed together invites sign errors and wrong combinations. Substitute completely first — reduce to one variable — then simplify once, cleanly.
Zone 2 · Multi-Relationship Word Problems

Three Sentences, Three Relationships, One Variable

Think about it
An amusement park sells Saturday and Sunday tickets. Saturday sales were 4 times Sunday sales. Together the two days sold 30,000 tickets. If you tried to give BOTH quantities their own separate letter, how many equations would you need? What if you gave only Sunday a letter and built Saturday FROM it?
The rule, every time
Write "let ___ = ___" first. Every other relationship gets built FROM that one letter.
Why this is the clever way
Picking the SMALLER, plainest quantity as your letter (here, Sunday, not Saturday) keeps every other relationship a simple multiple — "4 times S" instead of "a quarter of Sat." One letter, one equation, one solve. Two letters would need two equations and a substitution step you don't need.
Zone 2 · Deep Dive

The Four-Line Method, Applied Fully

1
Define the variable. "Let S = number of Sunday tickets." Always pick the quantity every other sentence refers back to.
2
Write each relationship as its own equation. "Saturday = 4 times Sunday" → 4S. "Together they total 30,000" → S + 4S = 30{,}000.
3
Solve. 5S = 30{,}000 → S = 6{,}000. Saturday = 4(6{,}000) = 24{,}000.
4
Circle what the question actually asks for — then answer THAT. If a weekday sells a third of Sunday's total: 6{,}000 ÷ 3 = 2{,}000. The same setup could ask for S, 4S, or this weekday value — three different correct final numbers from one setup.
Why Step 4 matters most
Most setup errors on the SHSAT aren't algebra mistakes — they're answering with the letter's value when the question wanted a DIFFERENT quantity built from it. Circling the target BEFORE computing catches this every time.
Zone 2 · Practice, Set 1

Multi-Relationship — Easy to Hard, Part 1

7Level 1
Two numbers have a sum of 40. The larger is 3 times the smaller. Write "let ___ = ___" for the smaller number, then find both numbers.
8Level 2
A bookstore sold twice as many paperbacks as hardcovers, and 240 books total. Define the variable for hardcovers first — how many paperbacks were sold?
9Level 2
Maya has 5 more than 3 times as many stickers as Lena. Together they have 65 stickers. Which quantity should get the letter — Maya's or Lena's — to keep the algebra simplest?
10Level 3
A theater sold adult and child tickets. Adult sales were 4 times child sales, and together they totaled 30,000 tickets — same setup as today's park problem. If a weekday sells one-third of the child total, how many weekday tickets are sold? Circle exactly which value the question wants before you answer.
SHSAT Trap: Solving for the Variable, Answering the Wrong Thing
The letter you solve for is rarely the final answer by itself. After solving, go back to the question and circle exactly what noun and unit it's asking for — Saturday tickets, weekday tickets, the larger number — then compute that specific value.
Zone 2 · Practice, Set 2

Multi-Relationship — Easy to Hard, Part 2

11Level 3
A parking lot has cars and motorcycles. There are 5 fewer motorcycles than twice the number of cars. Together there are 100 vehicles, and each car has 4 wheels while each motorcycle has 2. How many total wheels are in the lot? (Two relationships stack: count, then wheels.)
12Level 4
A concert sold VIP, floor, and balcony tickets: floor sales were 3 times VIP sales, and balcony sales were 2,000 more than floor sales. All three totaled 22,000 tickets. Write one "let" line, then build the other two entirely from it — what is the number of balcony tickets sold?
13Level 4
A charity race had walkers and runners. Runners were 4 times the walkers minus 10. Combined, they raised $30 per walker and $50 per runner for a total of $19,000, with 340 total participants. Set up both the count equation and the money equation from one "let" line — how many runners were there?
SHSAT Trap: Skipping the Variable-Definition Line
When a problem stacks three relationships, skipping straight to arithmetic almost always collapses two relationships into one by accident. The four-line habit — define, write each relationship as its own equation, solve, circle the target — is slower for 5 seconds and faster for the whole problem.
Zone 3 · Inequality ReviewThe Mirror

Dividing by a Negative Flips the World

Think about it
You know 2 < 5 is true. Multiply both sides by −1: is −2 < −5 still true, or does the relationship reverse? What does that tell you about what has to happen to the inequality SIGN every time you multiply or divide by a negative?
The rule, every time
Multiply or divide by a negative → flip EVERY inequality sign in the statement.
Why this is the clever way
Negative numbers reverse order on the number line — bigger negative numbers are actually smaller values. The sign flip isn't a memorized trick; it's the only way to keep the statement mathematically true after multiplying by something negative. Test it with 2 < 5 anytime you doubt yourself.
Zone 3 · Deep Dive

Compound Inequality, Solved and Sketched

1
Start: −5 ≤ 1 − 3x ≤ 4. Treat all three parts as one sandwich — every operation happens to ALL THREE at once.
2
Subtract 1 from all three parts: −6 ≤ −3x ≤ 3. No sign flip yet — subtraction never flips inequalities.
3
Divide all three parts by −3 — the dangerous step: because −3 is negative, BOTH signs flip direction: 2 ≥ x ≥ −1.
4
Rewrite left-to-right, smallest to largest: −1 ≤ x ≤ 2. This ordering habit prevents second-guessing which direction the circles point.
5
Sketch it — closed circles at both ends since the signs are ≤, meaning −1 and 2 are included.
Catch the trap before it catches you
If you divide by −3 and FORGET to flip, you'd get −2 ≤ x ≤ 1 — the exact mirror image, always sitting among the answer choices. Sketching the line forces you to notice which side the circles actually land on.
Zone 3 · Practice

Inequalities — Easy to Hard, Number Line Required

14Level 1
Solve and sketch: 3x + 4 < 19. Open or closed circle — which one, and why?
15Level 2
Solve and sketch: −2x + 5 ≥ 11. Before dividing, predict out loud: will the sign flip?
16Level 3
Solve and sketch: −3 < 2x − 7 ≤ 5. Compound — isolate the middle term the same way on all three parts at once.
17Level 4
Solve and sketch: −5 ≤ 1 − 3x ≤ 4 — today's deep-dive example. Rebuild all five steps from memory, then check: does your final line match the correct or the mirror-trap answer?
SHSAT Trap: The Mirror
A compound inequality solved without flipping both signs produces a number line that is the exact mirror image of the correct one — and that mirror is always sitting among the answer choices, waiting for the forgotten flip.
