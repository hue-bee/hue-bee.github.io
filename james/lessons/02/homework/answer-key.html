Part A — Repeated Growth (Compounding)

Problems 1–4

Big idea: a repeated percent change is never "add the same amount every time." Each period's change is computed on whatever the value became after the previous period — so multiply the growth factor by itself, once per period.

Problem 1 L1

$100 grows 10% each year for 2 years. Balance after 2 years?

1. Build the multiplier: growing by 10% means multiplying by 1.10. 2. Apply it twice: 100 × 1.10 × 1.10 = 100 × 1.10². 3. Compute: 1.10² = 1.21, so 100 × 1.21 = 121.
Answer: $121
SHSAT shortcut: Never compute "10% of 100, twice, then add" — that gives 120, the classic simple-interest trap answer that shows up as a wrong choice on purpose.
Problem 2 L2

Population of 200 grows 15%/year for 3 years. Correct setup: 200 × 1.15³, or 200 × (1.15 × 3)?

1. Identify the operation: "per year, for 3 years" means the rate is reapplied to a new base each year — that's exponentiation, not multiplication of the rate itself. 2. Correct setup: 200 × 1.15³ (multiply the growth factor by itself 3 times). 3. Compute: 1.15³ = 1.520875, so 200 × 1.520875 ≈ 304.18 → 304 people.
Answer: 200 × 1.15³ ≈ 304
Why the second setup is wrong: 200 × (1.15 × 3) triples the rate itself instead of compounding it — that's not how "each year" growth works, and it wildly overstates the result.
Problem 3 L3

A machine worth $8,000 depreciates 12%/year. Value after 2 years?

1. Depreciation multiplier: losing 12% means keeping 88%, so the multiplier is 0.88 — below 1, because value is shrinking. 2. Apply it twice: 8,000 × 0.88² = 8,000 × 0.7744. 3. Compute: 8,000 × 0.7744 = 6,195.20.
Answer: $6,195.20
Depreciation still compounds: the second year's 12% loss is computed on the already-smaller value, so the dollar amount lost shrinks each year even though the rate stays the same.
Problem 4 L4

A club of 50 grows 40% in year 1, then 20% (of the new total) in year 2. Membership after year 2?

1. Year 1: 50 × 1.40 = 70 members. 2. Year 2: apply 20% to the NEW total, 70, not to the original 50: 70 × 1.20 = 84. 3. Never combine the rates: 40% + 20% = 60% of 50 is a decoy — different rates, applied one at a time, in order, to a moving base.
Answer: 84 members
Trap: 50 × 1.60 = 80 is the "just add the percents" wrong answer — it ignores that the second rate applies to 70, not 50.

Part B — Percent Vocabulary: Value or Difference?

Problems 5–8

Classify before you calculate. "FROM" points to a starting value; "TO" points to an ending value; "how much/how many more" asks for the gap between two values, not either value itself.

Problem 5 L1

"The price increased TO $84." Value or difference — what do you circle?

1. Read the direction word: "TO" points forward, at the ending amount. 2. Classify: this is a value question — the answer is a single number, not a gap.
Answer: Value — circle 84
Problem 6 L1

"How much lower was Tuesday's temperature than Monday's?" Value or difference?

1. Read the phrase: "how much lower ... than" compares two values and asks for the gap between them. 2. Classify: this is a difference question — subtract, don't report either day's temperature alone.
Answer: Difference
Problem 7 L2

"Interest rates rose from 3% to 5%." Is that a "2 percent" increase or a "2 percentage point" increase — same thing?

1. Percentage points: 5 − 3 = 2 percentage points — a direct subtraction of the two percent figures. 2. Percent increase (relative): the change (2) divided by the original base (3): 2/3 ≈ 66.7%. 3. These are not the same thing: "2 percentage points" and "about 67% increase" both describe the identical rate change, using different denominators.
Answer: 2 percentage points ≠ 2 percent (it's ≈66.7% relative increase)
SHSAT shortcut: Whenever a question mixes two percentages with "rose from ___% to ___%," check whether it wants the point gap (just subtract) or the relative percent change (subtract, then divide by the original).
Problem 8 L3

"Membership increased FROM 40 by 25%." Which number is the "FROM," and value or resulting total?

1. Identify the FROM: "FROM 40" marks 40 as the starting base — the number the 25% is taken of. 2. Build the resulting total: 40 × 1.25 = 50 is the new value, if that's what's being asked. 3. Classify: the starting number (40) is fixed information, not the unknown — the question is really asking for the ending value, 50.
Answer: 40 is the FROM (base); resulting value = 50

Part C — Compounding & Vocabulary, Timed

Problems 9–15

Same two skills, mixed and against the clock. Read the whole stem before touching a calculator — the wording, not the presence of a percent sign, decides whether you compound, add, or subtract.

Problem 9 L2

A $40 phone plan rises 5%/year for 2 years. Price after 2 years?

1. Multiplier: 1.05, applied twice: 40 × 1.05². 2. Compute: 1.05² = 1.1025, so 40 × 1.1025 = 44.10.
Answer: $44.10
Problem 10 L3

A town of 3,000 adds exactly 200 residents/year for 4 years. Compounding or simple addition — and final population?

1. Read the wording: "exactly 200 new residents each year" is a flat amount, not a percent — that is simple addition, not compounding. 2. Compute: 3,000 + 4 × 200 = 3,000 + 800 = 3,800.
Answer: Simple addition; 3,800 residents
Trap: Seeing "each year for 4 years" and reflexively reaching for an exponent — compounding only happens when the growth is a percent of a changing base, not a fixed head count.
Problem 11 L4

$500 grows 8%/year for 3 years. How much MORE is that than growing by a flat $40/year (8% of the original) for 3 years?

1. Compounding path: 500 × 1.08³ = 500 × 1.259712 ≈ 629.86. 2. Simple path: 500 + 3 × 40 = 500 + 120 = 620. 3. Subtract: 629.86 − 620 = 9.86.
Answer: ≈$9.86 more
Why so small: over only 3 years at a modest rate, compounding and simple growth are close — the visible gap grows much larger with more years or a higher rate. Don't let closeness in size tempt you into thinking the two methods are "basically the same."
Problem 12 L2 · 75s

$30 doubles each year for 2 years. Value now?

1. "Doubled" = increased 100%: multiplier is 2.00, not 1.00 (1.00 would mean no change). 2. Apply twice: 30 × 2 × 2 = 120.
Answer: $120
Problem 13 L3 · 75s

Attendance rose FROM 80 TO 92. Percent increase?

1. Find the change: 92 − 80 = 12. 2. Base is the FROM number: circle 80, not 92 — percent increase always divides by the starting value. 3. Compute: 12/80 = 0.15 = 15%.
Answer: 15% increase
Trap: Dividing 12 by 92 instead of 80 gives ≈13%, a common wrong-base answer choice.
Problem 14 L4 · 75s

Approval rating went from 42% to 47%. How many percentage points did it rise?

1. Percentage points = direct subtraction: 47 − 42 = 5. 2. No division needed: "percentage points" never asks for a relative percent — that would require dividing by 42.
Answer: 5 percentage points
Problem 15 L4 · 75s

A 200 sq ft garden grows 10%/year for 2 years. How much LARGER after year 2 than after year 1?

1. After year 1: 200 × 1.10 = 220. 2. After year 2: 220 × 1.10 = 242. 3. This asks for the gap, not the total: 242 − 220 = 22.
Answer: 22 sq ft larger
Trap: Reporting 242 (the year-2 total) instead of 22 (the requested year-over-year gap) is exactly the "value vs. difference" mistake from Part B, disguised inside a growth problem.

Part D — ELA: Paragraph Job Notes

Problems 16–18

Answer strictly from the job-note phrases, not from rereading. Each paragraph's "job" is its function in the argument — introduce, complicate, extend, counter, conclude — not a summary of its content.

Passage recap (5 paragraphs): ¶1 sets up the growth-mindset belief and hints the story is more complicated · ¶2 explains Dweck's original findings and their popularization · ¶3 presents replication studies that found small/inconsistent effects (the counterexample to the popularized claim) · ¶4 applies the lesson to how job notes are taught in this course · ¶5 generalizes the lesson beyond this one classroom skill.
Problem 16 L2

Which paragraph's job is to "introduce the counterexample"?

1. Scan job notes only: look for the paragraph whose phrase signals pushback against the popularized claim. 2. Match: Paragraph 3 reports replication studies showing "only small, inconsistent effects" — that's the counterexample to Paragraph 2's popularized version of Dweck's findings.
Answer: Paragraph 3
Problem 17 L3

What job does the final paragraph perform — conclude, qualify, or pivot?

1. Check what the paragraph actually does: it does not just restate the classroom point — it says "the broader lesson extends past this single classroom skill." 2. Classify the move: that's a pivot/extension — broadening the specific lesson (job notes) into a general principle (act instead of waiting on belief), not a simple summary.
Answer: Pivots — it extends/generalizes the lesson, rather than merely concluding
Why this matters: "Conclude" implies restating what came before; a paragraph that introduces a new, wider claim is doing a different job, even if it comes last.
Problem 18 L4

Which two paragraphs' jobs are most similar to each other, based only on job-note phrases?

1. Compare functions, not topics: Paragraph 1 opens by claiming the popular story is "more complicated than this slogan implies" — a general framing move. 2. Paragraph 5 closes by generalizing the specific classroom lesson into a wider principle — also a general framing move, at the opposite end of the passage. 3. Shared job: both paragraphs step back from the specific case to make a broader claim about the underlying issue — one at the start, one at the end.
Answer: Paragraphs 1 and 5 — both "broaden to the general principle"
Why job notes catch this: A summary of content would never link the opening and closing paragraphs, since they discuss different specifics (mindset research vs. job-notes strategy). Only a function-based note reveals they're doing the same rhetorical job.

Tutor Notes — High-Value Habits

  • Repeated growth: build the multiplier once, then apply it once per period by raising it to a power — never multiply the rate itself by the number of periods.
  • Simple vs. compound: a flat dollar/head-count amount added each period is simple addition; a percent of a changing base is compounding. The wording decides, not the presence of a percent sign.
  • Value vs. difference: "FROM/TO" and "how much more/less" are the tell. Classify which one is being asked for before computing anything.
  • Percentage points vs. percent: subtracting two percentages gives percentage points; dividing that gap by the original percentage gives the relative percent change. They are never interchangeable.
  • Job notes: answer purpose questions from the function-phrase only. A paragraph's job is what it does in the argument, not what it says.
