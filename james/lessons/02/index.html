Zone 1 · Repeated Growth

Compounding Beats Addition — Why?

Think about it
A value of 50 grows 40% every year for 3 years. In year 2, does that 40% get applied to the original 50, or to whatever the value became after year 1? What happens to the SIZE of each year's growth as a result?
Right way vs. wrong way, side by side
50 × 1.4³ = 137.2 → 137   vs.   50 + 3(20) = 110 (wrong)
Zone 1 · Practice

Repeated Growth — Easy to Hard

1Level 1
A savings account starts at $100 and grows 10% each year for 2 years. What is the balance after 2 years? Write the multiplier first, then apply it twice.
2Level 2
A population of 200 increases by 15% per year for 3 years. Is the correct setup 200 × 1.15³, or 200 × (1.15 × 3)? Say why before computing.
3Level 3
A machine's value depreciates 12% per year. If it's worth $8,000 now, what will it be worth in 2 years? Depreciation still compounds — what should the multiplier be, above or below 1?
4Level 4
A club has 50 members. Membership grows 40% in year 1, then an additional 20% in year 2 (of the NEW total, not the original 50). What is the membership after year 2? Two different rates, same compounding logic — apply them one at a time, in order.
Zone 2 · Percent VocabularyNew Skill

Classify Before You Calculate: Value or Difference?

Think about it
"Sales increased FROM $200" points backward to a starting value. "Sales increased TO $200" points forward to an ending value. Read each stem below — which direction is it pointing, and is the final answer a single number or a gap between two numbers?
The stem words that decide everything
"increase FROM" → original value. "increase TO" → new value. "how much lower/higher" → a difference. "percentage point" → a raw gap between two percents, not a percent-of-a-percent.
Zone 2 · Practice — Classify, Don't Solve

Eight Stems — Value or Difference?

5Level 1
"The price increased TO $84." Value, or difference? What number would you circle as the answer?
6Level 1
"How much lower was Tuesday's temperature than Monday's?" Value, or difference?
7Level 2
"Interest rates rose from 3% to 5%." Is that a "2 percent" increase or a "2 percentage point" increase — and are those the same thing?
8Level 3
"Membership increased FROM 40 members by 25%." Which number is the "FROM," and does the question want that starting number or the resulting total?
Percentage point vs. percent
Going from 3% to 5% is a rise of 2 percentage points, but it's a 2/3 ≈ 66.7% relative percent increase. Both descriptions are true. Only one matches what a given question asks for.
Zone 3 · Catch the TutorHighest-Yield Drill

Watch the Tutor Solve This — Then Fix It

The tutor's work (on purpose, wrong)
"A club has 60 members. Membership grows 15% every year for 3 years. So: 15% of 60 is 9. Three years of that is 27. 60 + 27 = 87 members." Where exactly did that reasoning go wrong — and what should have happened to the 15% in year 2?
The fix, once you say it
Year 2's 15% belongs to year 1's NEW total, not the original 60. Correct: 60 × 1.15³ = 91.29 → 91 members. The tutor's 87 is a real, commonly offered wrong choice — it's what "compound growth mistaken for simple growth" always produces.
Zone 3 · Practice

Compounding or Simple? — Read Before You Compute

9Level 2
A phone plan's price rises 5% every year for 2 years, starting at $40. What's the price after 2 years? Multiplier first, then apply it twice — not "add 5% of 40 twice."
10Level 3
A town added exactly 200 new residents each year for 4 years, starting at 3,000. Is this compounding, or simple addition — and what's the population after 4 years? The wording decides the method, not the presence of a percent sign.
11Level 4
An investment of $500 grows 8% per year for 3 years. How much MORE is it worth than if it had simply grown by a flat $40 per year (8% of the original) for those same 3 years? Solve both paths, then subtract.
The one question that sorts every one of these
Does each period's growth get computed on the ORIGINAL number, or on whatever the number became last period? "Each year," "compounded," and "grows by r% per year" signal compounding. A flat dollar amount added "each year" signals simple addition.

ELA · The Audit

Your Own Words Were Already Right

Look at your 1A homework margin notes
For each purpose question, you wrote your own answer BEFORE looking at choices. Now compare: did your own phrasing ever describe the correct job accurately, and then you still picked a different, wrong choice? If so — why did you talk yourself out of your own correct instinct?
The pattern worth circling
If your margin note matches the right answer's meaning but you selected something else, that's not a content gap — it's a confidence and matching problem. That gap is exactly what today's job-notes drill is built to close.
ELA · New Tool

One Phrase Per Paragraph, Almost No Content Words

Think about it
If you wrote down every fact in a 5-paragraph passage, you'd have a second passage — not a tool. What's the smallest possible label for "what job does paragraph 3 do" that still lets you answer a question later without rereading?
The paragraph job note
A phrase like "gives counterexample," "shifts to decline," or "introduces expert" — a job, in 2–4 words, with almost no content words from the passage itself. If your note contains a name, date, or number, it's a summary note, not a job note.
ELA · Worked Example

Answer From the Notes. Don't Reread.

Practice on a 5-paragraph passage
Write your job note for each paragraph as you read it — once, moving forward, never doubling back. When you finish, cover the passage completely. Using only your five phrases, answer: "which paragraph's job is to challenge the claim made in paragraph 2?"
Why this works on the real test
The Fall 2026 test won't let you scroll back once a passage-set is submitted for other item types, and rereading burns your two-minute cap. A passage you've already labeled by function is a passage you can answer from without ever touching it again.
Check your note, not your memory
If you catch yourself rereading a paragraph to answer a question, your job note for that paragraph wasn't specific enough — sharpen it next time, don't just reread and move on.
ELA · Practice — From Job Notes Only

Three Purpose Questions — No Rereading Allowed

16Level 2
Using only your job notes: which paragraph's job is to "introduce the counterexample"? Answer before glancing back at the passage.
17Level 3
What job does the final paragraph perform for the passage as a whole — does it conclude, qualify, or pivot? Say the job before checking choices.
18Level 4
Which two paragraphs' jobs are most similar to each other, based only on your phrases — not on subject matter? Explain the shared job in one sentence.
Recap · Building on Week 1's Traps

Old Traps, New Guardrails

Flat vs. Compounding — Math
Any time growth repeats "per year" or "each period," check whether the base changes each time. If it does, multiply the growth factor by itself — never add the same flat amount more than once.
Value or Difference — Math
This is "Right Math, Wrong Line" wearing new vocabulary. "From/to," "how much," and "percentage point" all decide whether the final answer is a single number or a gap.
Why the audit mattered today
If your own written instinct was already correct on some 1A homework items, "True But Useless" isn't a content problem for you — it's a confidence problem. Job notes give you a written anchor so you stop overriding a correct first instinct.
