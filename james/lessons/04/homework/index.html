Part C — ELA: Author's Purpose & Tone

Why the Sign Flips: The Hidden Symmetry Behind Inequalities

[¶1] When students first meet inequalities, they are usually handed a rule to memorize: multiply or divide by a negative number, and the sign flips. Few teachers explain why. The reason is not a trick of algebra but a simple fact about how negative numbers reorder the number line — and once a student sees it, the rule stops feeling arbitrary.

[¶2] Consider the true statement 2 < 5. Multiply both sides by −1, and you get −2 and −5. However, −2 is actually greater than −5, because −2 sits to the right of −5 on the number line. The original relationship — "less than" — no longer holds unless the inequality symbol flips to match the new order. Multiplying by a negative number is really a reflection: it flips every point on the number line across zero, and reflections reverse order.

[¶3] Addition and subtraction, by contrast, do not reflect anything. Adding 3 to both sides of 2 < 5 simply slides both numbers the same distance in the same direction; their relative order never changes. As a result, no flip is ever needed for addition or subtraction, no matter how large or small the number being added is.

[¶4] This distinction matters enormously on tests like the SHSAT, where compound inequalities often bury a negative coefficient in the middle of a multi-step problem. A student who has memorized "multiply by negative, flip" without understanding the reflection behind it will often forget to flip when the negative sign is disguised inside a fraction or a two-step expression.

[¶5] Ultimately, understanding the flip rule as a reflection — not a memorized trick — turns a fragile shortcut into permanent number sense. Students who see why the rule works rarely forget it under exam pressure, because they are no longer relying on memory alone; they are reasoning from a picture of the number line itself.
