Sprint · Mental Math Warm-Up

Two Percents That Feel Impossible Until They're Not

Think first: 0.5% of 480
1% of 480 is 4.8. Half of that percent means half that answer. What is 0.5% of 480? Do it without a calculator — decide what "half of 1%" actually asks you to do to 4.8.
Think first: 250% of 60
100% of 60 is just 60. 250% is two and a half times that whole amount. What is 60 × 2.5? Notice that a percent bigger than 100 always means "more than the original."
Your Habit Target for This Sprint
Percents under 1% shrink the answer far below what your gut expects. Percents over 100% grow the answer past the original number. Say out loud, before you compute: "this result should be smaller / bigger than the original" — then check your answer against that prediction.
Math Zone 1 · Percent of a Derived QuantityCore Skill

A Rectangle Grows in Two Directions at Once

Think about it
A field is 80 meters by 50 meters. It grows to 92 meters by 60 meters. The length grew 15% and the width grew 20%. Can you just add 15% + 20% to get the percent the AREA grew? Why would that double-count something?
Compute the actual area before and after — never combine percents from two different dimensions
80 × 50 = 4,000  →  92 × 60 = 5,520
The actual percent increase
Change ÷ original = (5,520 − 4,000) ÷ 4,000 = 1,520 ÷ 4,000 = 0.38 = 38% increase. That's larger than 15% + 20% = 35% because growing both dimensions together creates extra "bonus" area neither dimension alone accounts for.
Math Zone 1 · Demolishing the Wrong Paths

Three Ways to Get This Problem Wrong

Trap 1: "It's 15%"
This is just the length's growth (80 → 92 is 15%). It ignores that the width ALSO grew. Area depends on both dimensions — using only one throws away real information.
Trap 2: "Width grew 10"
50 → 60 is a change of 10 meters, but 10 is a length, not a percent. Mixing a raw unit (meters) into a percent answer confuses "how much" with "what fraction."
Trap 3: "15% + 20% = 35%"
Adding the two side-percentages ignores the cross-term created when both sides grow together. The true 38% is always a bit more than the simple sum.
The Rule That Fixes All Three
Compute the actual before-quantity and the actual after-quantity as real numbers first. Only then apply percent change = (after − before) ÷ before. Never add or combine percents that came from two different dimensions — length percent and width percent are not interchangeable with area percent.
Math Zone 1 · Practice

Percent of a Derived Quantity — Easy to Hard

1Level 1
A rug is 6 ft by 4 ft (area 24 ft²). It's replaced by a 9 ft by 6 ft rug. Find the actual area of each rug first, then the percent increase.
2Level 2
A box's volume comes from length × width × height. If length grows 10% and width grows 10% but height stays the same, does volume grow exactly 20%? Compute with real numbers to check.
3. Level 4: A garden is 45 m by 30 m (area 1,350 m²). Its length increases to 54 m and its width increases to 36 m. What is the percent increase in area?
SHSAT Trap: Averaging the Two Side-Percents
54 ÷ 45 = 20% growth in length; 36 ÷ 30 = 20% growth in width. Averaging or adding gives 20% or 40% — both wrong. Real areas: 1,350 → 1,944; change ÷ original = 594 ÷ 1,350 = 44%.
Math Zone 2 · Fractional PercentsCore Skill

Percents Smaller Than One Whole Percent

Think about it
1% of a number means "1 out of 100 parts." What does ½% mean — is it 1 out of 100, or 1 out of 200? If ½ is half of 1, and 1% is 1/100, what fraction is half of 1/100?
Three forms of the same value
½% is a fraction of a percent. Converting to decimal: ½% = 0.5% = 0.005. Converting to fraction: 0.005 = 5/1000 = 1/200. All three — ½%, 0.005, and 1/200 — represent exactly the same value; the test can present any of the three and expect you to recognize the others instantly.
Math Zone 2 · Negation TrapHigh-Frequency

Named Trap: "Flip the Question"

Think about it
A question asks "which of the following is NOT equivalent to ½%?" If three choices are all secretly equal to ½% and one choice is different, which strategy is safer: checking every choice against ½%, or stopping the moment you find one that looks right?
Why This Trap Works
Your brain is trained to hunt for the ONE right answer and stop. Negation stems ("NOT," "EXCEPT," "which is FALSE") flip that instinct backward — now three answers are secretly correct and only one is different. Stopping early after finding "a match" costs you the item, because a match to ½% is a WRONG answer here.
The Fix: Check All Four, Every Time
On any negation stem, convert every single choice to the same form (usually decimal) before deciding. Example: ½% = 0.005. Choices might read 0.005, 1/200, 5/1000, and 0.05 — the first three all equal ½%; 0.05 = 5% is ten times too large and is the answer. You cannot find that without checking all four.
Math Zone 3 · Two-Stage Proportional Scale-UpCore Skill

Resolve the Percent Before You Scale the Population

Think about it
A sample survey shows 60% support, 25% oppose, and 15% undecided. Before scaling anything to the full population, what should happen to that 15%? If undecided voters split evenly when forced to choose, how much of that 15% moves to the "support" side?
Stage 1 — resolve the percent
Half the undecided 15% joins support: 60% + 7.5% = 67.5% adjusted support rate. This step happens entirely in percent-world — no population number needed yet.
Math Recap · Accuracy Habits

Three Habits That Prevent Every Trap Today

1. Compute, Don't Combine
Find the actual before and after values for derived quantities. Never add or average percents from two different dimensions.
2. Convert Every Choice
On negation stems ("NOT equivalent," "EXCEPT"), convert all four answers to the same form before deciding — never stop at the first match.
3. Resolve, Then Scale
Fix undecided percentages within percent-world first. Only multiply by the real population size in a separate, final step.
Bridge to ELA
The same discipline — read carefully, name the type of thing you're looking for, then check systematically — carries directly into the next 23 minutes. In ELA, instead of naming a percent trap, you'll be naming a rhetorical METHOD.
ELA · The "Through" RuleCore Skill

"Through" Always Asks HOW, Never WHAT

Think about it
"The author illustrates the character's loneliness THROUGH ___." Is the blank asking for a fact about the character, or a technique the author used to show that fact? If a choice just restates a plot detail with no technique named, can it ever be correct here?
The Hard Rule
When a stem contains "illustrated through," "conveyed through," or "developed through," the correct answer must NAME A METHOD — never a fact. A fact-only choice is always a trap, no matter how true it sounds.
ELA · Build the Physical Reference Card

The Six Methods — Memorize These Cold

Anecdote
A short story or single specific incident used to make a broader point real.
Comparison
Placing two things side by side (often with "like" or "as") to highlight a shared quality.
Sensory Description
Vivid detail appealing to sight, sound, touch, taste, or smell to make a scene vivid.
Contrast
Showing two things that differ sharply to sharpen the reader's sense of each one.
Chronology
Presenting events in time order so the reader tracks change or progression directly.
Example
A specific instance offered as concrete proof of a general claim already made.
Make It Physical
Write these six on an index card today. Keep it visible on the desk for the next four weeks — every "through" question becomes a matching exercise against this card, not a cold guess.
ELA · Diagnostic Miss 1 of 3

Lowell's Decline: Name the Method First

Reading Passage
By 1950, Lowell, Massachusetts had lost nearly forty percent of the textile jobs that had once made it the pride of American manufacturing. In 1920, the city's mills employed over 40,000 workers across two dozen brick factory buildings that lined the Merrimack River. By 1935, that number had fallen to 28,000. By 1950, fewer than 15,000 remained on any mill payroll, and by 1957 the last of the great mills, the Boott Cotton Mills, shut its doors for good. Where rows of workers once fed raw cotton into deafening looms from dawn until dusk, empty windows now looked out over silent floors. The city's tax base shrank in step with its workforce, and neighborhoods that had once bustled with mill families slowly emptied as young people left to find work elsewhere.
Think about it
The stem asks how the passage illustrates a city's economic decline "through" something. Before looking at any answer choice, what TYPE of thing must the correct answer contain? Scan the passage: does it show one dramatic before/after image, a string of specific numbers, or a step-by-step timeline of decline?
Two-Step Process, Every Time
Step 1 — Name the method BEFORE reading choices: if the passage traces the decline year by year, the method is likely chronology or comparison (then vs. now).
Step 2 — Match to a choice: only after naming it, scan the four choices for the one that states that same method — not a fact about Lowell.
Why This Was Missed
A wrong choice restated a true fact from the passage ("Lowell's mills closed") without naming any method. It felt right because it was factually accurate — but "accurate" and "answers the HOW question" are not the same thing.
ELA · Diagnostic Miss 2 of 3

Moccasin Mastery: Spotting Sensory Description

Reading Passage
Her hands moved across the deer hide with a certainty that seemed to bypass thought altogether. The leather, softened over days of careful working, gave way beneath her fingers like warm wax, releasing a faint smoky scent left over from the tanning fire. Each stitch she pulled through was even and tight, the sinew thread disappearing into the hide so cleanly that the seam looked almost invisible. The awl clicked softly against her thimble with every puncture, a small, steady rhythm that had not changed in the fifty years she had been making moccasins. When she finally held the finished pair up to the light, the beadwork along the vamp caught the sun in flashes of red and white, and the leather itself seemed to glow with the warm, worn-in color of something built to last.
Think about it
The passage describes a craftsperson's skill making moccasins "through" specific descriptive detail — texture of leather, precision of stitches, sound of tools. What method names "detail that appeals to the senses"? Is that different from an anecdote, which is a short STORY rather than a description?
The Distinction That Matters
Sensory description paints a scene with detail (texture, sound, sight) without necessarily telling a story. An anecdote is a brief NARRATIVE — something happens, in sequence. If the passage lingers on how something looks or feels rather than narrating an event, name it sensory description, not anecdote.
Why This Was Missed
The correct choice named "vivid sensory detail," but a nearby wrong choice named "a personal anecdote about her childhood" — a plausible-sounding method that was simply the wrong one for this passage's actual technique. Naming the method first, before reading choices, prevents this substitution.
ELA · Diagnostic Miss 3 of 3

Life & History Traveling Toward One Ending

Reading Passage
As a young clerk, Marcus had watched the shipping company he worked for grow from a single dock into an empire spanning three ports, and he had grown alongside it, moving from ledger boy to floor manager to junior partner in the span of twenty years. The company's fortunes rose through the 1880s just as his own did, each new contract mirrored by a promotion, each expansion mirrored by a raise. But when the depression of 1893 struck, the company's overextended loans came due all at once, and as its ships sat empty in harbor, Marcus watched his own savings, tied up in company stock, evaporate within a single financial quarter. By the time both the company and Marcus filed for what remained of their futures, it was clear that the two stories, one of commerce and one of a single life, had been telling the same story all along, arriving together at the same ruin.
Think about it
The passage says a character's personal life and a historical event are "developed through" being shown as parallel journeys heading toward the same conclusion. Is placing two separate storylines side-by-side to show a shared pattern a comparison, or is it chronology, since both involve time passing?
Resolving the Ambiguity
Chronology means ONE sequence of events in time order. Comparison means TWO things placed side by side to reveal a shared quality. Here, two separate tracks (a life, a historical era) run in parallel toward one shared ending — that structural pairing is comparison, even though each track individually unfolds in time.
The General Lesson
When a passage seems to fit two methods at once, ask which method describes the STRUCTURE the author chose (comparison = two things side by side) versus which method is just a background feature (chronology = time passing within each thing). The structural choice is almost always the intended answer.
ELA · Practice

Four New "Through" Items — Name It, Then Match It

1Level 1
Passage
The wind arrived first as a low moan through the eaves, then rose within minutes to a shriek that rattled the windows in their frames. Rain hit the roof so hard it sounded like gravel being poured from above, and somewhere out in the dark a tree limb cracked and fell with a sound like a gunshot. The power failed with a single flat pop, and the house went black except for the flashes of lightning strobing through the curtains.
The author conveys the storm's intensity through ___. Before reading choices: does the passage describe sounds/sights, or narrate a single dramatic moment?
2Level 2
Passage
Over the course of her career, Dr. Alvarez published forty-two papers, trained eleven graduate students, and secured six major research grants. But it was her third year in the lab, after her hypothesis had failed for the ninth consecutive trial, that revealed who she really was: rather than abandon the experiment, she redesigned the apparatus from scratch overnight and returned the next morning to run trial ten, which finally confirmed what she had suspected all along.
The passage illustrates the scientist's persistence through ___. Scan for a short story of one specific attempt versus a list of her many achievements.
3Level 3
Passage
On the east side of the river, property values had tripled in a decade as new cafes and renovated townhouses drew young professionals eager for short commutes. On the west side, in the same ten years, three factories had closed, the local school had lost half its enrollment, and for-sale signs outnumbered occupied storefronts along the main avenue. The two neighborhoods, separated by less than a mile of water, had become case studies in opposite directions.
The two neighborhoods' fates are developed through ___. Look for whether the author places them side by side or narrates one, then the other, in time order.
4Level 4
Passage
My grandmother kept a single wooden spoon on the kitchen wall, worn smooth and dark at the handle from three generations of hands. She never explained why she refused to replace it with any of the newer spoons gathering dust in the drawer. It was only after she passed that I understood: that spoon, chipped and stained as it was, held every meal she had ever cooked for a family that had crossed an ocean to build a new life, and to throw it away would have been to throw away all of it at once.
The essay's argument about tradition is conveyed through ___, where a single small object stands in for an entire cultural practice. Which method centers on ONE specific instance as proof?
Non-Negotiable Sequence
Name the method silently before reading any choice. Then and only then scan the four choices for the one naming that same method. Never let a true-sounding fact substitute for a named technique.
