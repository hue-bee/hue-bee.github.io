Zone 1 — Reverse Percent

Problems 1–4

Big idea: new = old × (1 ± r), so old = new ÷ (1 ± r). Never multiply the new value by the percent again — that only works forward, not backward.

Problem 1 L1

After a 20% increase, a number becomes 60. What was the original number?

1. Choose the right factor: "increase" means the multiplier is 1.20, not 0.80 (0.80 is for a decrease). 2. Set up the equation: 60 = 1.20 × old. 3. Divide, don't multiply back: old = 60 ÷ 1.20 = 50.
Answer: 50
Clever move: Say "increase → multiply by 1-point-something" out loud before writing any number. Locking in the factor first prevents flipping it under pressure.
Problem 2 L2

A shirt's price dropped 15% to $42.50. What was the original price?

1. Sign check: a drop means the factor is (1 − 0.15) = 0.85. 2. Set up the equation: 42.50 = 0.85 × old. 3. Divide: old = 42.50 ÷ 0.85 = 50.
Answer: $50.00
SHSAT trap: A common wrong move is finding 15% of $42.50 and adding it back (42.50 + 6.375 = $48.88) — that treats the new price as if it were the base, which it isn't. The 15% always applies to the original price, which is unknown.
Problem 3 L3

Tuesday's temperature was 84°, which is 5% higher than Monday's. What was Monday's temperature?

1. Build the equation directly: 84 = 1.05 × x, where x = Monday's temperature. 2. Isolate x: x = 84 ÷ 1.05. 3. Compute: x = 80.
Answer: 80°
Why the equation matters: Skipping straight to "5% of 84 is 4.2, so subtract" gives 79.8° — close, but wrong, because 5% of Monday's temperature is not the same as 5% of Tuesday's. Always build 84 = 1.05x, never estimate off the wrong base.
Problem 4 L4

A price of $63 already includes a 10% markup over wholesale cost. Find the wholesale cost, then find the price after a separate 10% discount off that wholesale cost.

1. Reverse the markup: 63 = 1.10 × wholesale, so wholesale = 63 ÷ 1.10 ≈ $57.27. 2. Reset the base: the discount applies to the wholesale cost you just found, not to $63. 3. Apply the discount forward: $57.27 × 0.90 ≈ $51.55.
Answer: Wholesale ≈ $57.27; discounted price ≈ $51.55
Two-reversal habit: Treat this as two separate mini-problems joined by one sentence. Finish and label the first answer (wholesale) completely before touching the second calculation — mixing the steps is where multi-step percent problems fall apart.

Zone 2 — Value or Difference?

Problems 5–6

Big idea: solving correctly is only half the job. Before circling an answer, finish the sentence "the question wants ___" — value, or difference. The SHSAT deliberately offers the other one as a wrong choice.

Problem 5 L1

A club had 25 members. Membership grew 20%. How many members does the club have now?

1. Identify the target: the question asks for the new value, not the amount of growth. 2. Apply the growth factor: 25 × 1.20 = 30.
Answer: 30 members
Clever move: Since this one truly does ask for the new value, 30 is correct as-is — but get in the habit of checking anyway. On the next problem, the wording flips.
Problem 6 L3

Monday's attendance was 150 people. Tuesday's attendance was 10% lower. How much lower, in people, was Tuesday than Monday?

1. Find Tuesday's value first: 150 × 0.90 = 135. 2. Reread the target: the question wants the difference (how much lower), not Tuesday's attendance itself. 3. Subtract: 150 − 135 = 15.
Answer: 15 people
SHSAT trap — Right Math, Wrong Line: A rushed student solves correctly, gets 135, and circles it — but 135 is Tuesday's attendance, not "how much lower." The wrong choice 135 is placed on purpose. Circle the actual target word ("lower") before you touch the answer choices.

Zone 3 — Complement Percent

Problems 7–8

Big idea: 100% − given % tells you what's missing. Convert the complement to a decimal before touching the fraction — decimal drift is where these problems get stolen.

Problem 7 L1

80% of a class passed a quiz. What fraction did NOT pass?

1. Find the complement: 100% − 80% = 20%. 2. Convert to a fraction: 20% = 20/100 = 1/5.
Answer: 1/5
Habit: Always start from 100%, not from 80%. The question is about the missing piece, not the given piece.
Problem 8 L3

99.7% of parts passed inspection. What fraction of parts did NOT pass? (Watch for the traps 3/100 and 3/10.)

1. Find the complement: 100% − 99.7% = 0.3%. 2. Convert to a decimal carefully: 0.3% = 0.003. 3. Convert to a fraction: 0.003 = 3/1000.
Answer: 3/1000
SHSAT trap — Decimal Drift: 3/100 is actually 3% and 3/10 is actually 30% — both are one decimal place off from 0.3%. Convert the percent to a decimal first (0.003), then count the zeroes into the denominator. Never eyeball a small percent straight into a fraction.

Zone 3 — Mixed Reverse & Complement (Timed)

Problems 9–12

Big idea: the last four problems combine everything above under a 75-second cap. Identify which skill applies — reverse percent, or complement percent, or both plus a value-vs-difference check — before writing a single number.

Problem 9 L2

A number increased by 8% is 108. What was the original number?

1. Set up the equation: 108 = 1.08 × old. 2. Divide: old = 108 ÷ 1.08 = 100.
Answer: 100
Speed tip: Recognizing 1.08 as the factor should take one second flat — increase, so add r to 1. Don't pause to "think it through" each time; the template is automatic by now.
Problem 10 L3 · 75s

99.5% of a shipment arrived undamaged. What fraction arrived damaged?

1. Find the complement: 100% − 99.5% = 0.5%. 2. Convert: 0.5% = 0.005 = 5/1000 = 1/200.
Answer: 1/200
Trap avoided: A rushed answer of 1/20 (which is 5%, not 0.5%) is the classic decimal-drift trap here — one zero makes a ten-times difference.
Problem 11 L4 · 75s

Friday's sales were 12% below Thursday's, and Thursday's were $250. How much MORE did Thursday sell than Friday, in dollars?

1. Find Friday's value: 250 × 0.88 = 220. 2. Circle the target: the question wants the difference, not Friday's sales figure. 3. Subtract: 250 − 220 = 30.
Answer: $30
SHSAT trap: $220 (Friday's sales) is a tempting wrong choice sitting right there for anyone who stops one step too early. "How much MORE" always signals a subtraction is still coming.
Problem 12 L4 · 75s

99.8% of a batch of 4,000 bulbs worked. How many bulbs did NOT work?

1. Find the complement: 100% − 99.8% = 0.2%. 2. Convert to a fraction or decimal: 0.2% = 0.002. 3. Multiply by the total — don't skip this step: 4,000 × 0.002 = 8.
Answer: 8 bulbs
Where students slip: Finding the fraction (1/500) and stopping there answers a different question. The problem asks for a bulb count, so the fraction is only the middle step, not the final answer.

ELA — Function of a Detail

Questions 13–16

Big idea: a purpose answer names a job (a verb — to establish, to contrast, to foreshadow), not a summary of what the detail literally says. Cover the choices, say the job out loud in your own words, then reveal the choices and match.

Q13.A passage opens with a short anecdote about a single factory worker before discussing the broader industry. What job does that opening anecdote perform? L1
Model answer: The anecdote's job is to make a large, impersonal topic (an entire industry) feel concrete and human before zooming out to statistics and trends. It is a classic "personal example before the big picture" move — it earns the reader's attention and gives an abstract subject a human face.
SHSAT trap: A wrong choice will often just restate what the worker did (a summary). The right choice names the anecdote's function — "to introduce the topic on a personal scale" — not the worker's actions themselves.
Q14.A paragraph lists three statistics about population growth right after a claim that a city was "booming." What job do those statistics do for the claim just made? L2
Model answer: The statistics' job is to support and quantify the claim that the city was "booming" — they turn a vague, subjective word into something measurable and credible. This is a classic claim-then-evidence structure.
SHSAT trap: Don't pick a choice that says the statistics "introduce a new idea." They don't introduce anything new — they back up the idea the author already stated.
Q15.A sentence describes a mill town as having "leather-headedness — ignorance and idiocy." Is the job here to give a neutral fact, or to reveal the author's attitude? L3
Model answer: The job is to reveal the author's harsh, judgmental attitude toward the town — not to state a neutral fact. Loaded, insulting language ("ignorance and idiocy") is a strong signal of tone/attitude, never a neutral description.
SHSAT trap — true but useless: A choice that says "the sentence describes the town's residents" is technically true but useless — it restates content instead of naming the tone-revealing job the sentence is actually doing. True-but-useless choices are designed to catch students who don't ask "what job."
Q16.A closing sentence states that "his life, like his city's history, seemed to travel toward one ending." What job does this comparison do at the end of a biographical passage? L4
Model answer: The comparison's job is to tie the individual's fate to the larger fate of the city, giving the passage a unifying, reflective close — linking a personal story to a broader historical arc is a common way biographical passages create a sense of closure and significance at the very end.
Habit check: Closing-line questions almost never ask "what does this literally say" — they ask what effect the ending creates. Name the job (unify, conclude, reflect) before you look at a single answer choice.

Common Errors This Session

  • Flipping the factor: using 0.80 for an increase or 1.20 for a decrease — say "increase or decrease" out loud before writing the multiplier.
  • Solving for the wrong target: circling the new value when the question asks for the difference, or vice versa — always finish "the question wants ___" before answering.
  • Decimal drift on small percents: turning 0.3% or 0.5% into 3/100 or 5/100 instead of 3/1000 or 5/1000 — convert to a decimal first, then count zeroes.
  • Stopping at the fraction: when a problem asks for a count (like "how many bulbs"), the complement fraction is a middle step, not the final answer.
  • Summarizing instead of naming the job: on ELA purpose questions, restating what a detail says instead of naming what it does for the passage.
