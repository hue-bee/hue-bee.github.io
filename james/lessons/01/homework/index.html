Part E — ELA: Function of a Detail

6 Questions · ~15 min

Cover the answer choices with your hand before reading them. Say the sentence's JOB out loud in your own words — then reveal the choices and match.

Trap name — True But Useless: One answer choice will accurately restate the sentence's content. It will be completely true — and completely wrong, because the question asked what the sentence DOES, not what it SAYS.
Why the Grid Beat the Circle: The Story of the Manhattan Street Plan

[¶1] In 1811, a small commission of surveyors sat down to decide the future shape of a growing American city. Rather than the winding, organic streets common to older European cities, they chose something radically plain: a grid of straight lines and right angles, stretching uniformly across the island. Critics at the time called it monotonous and unimaginative. They were, in a narrow sense, correct — and that was exactly the point.

[¶2] A grid's greatest strength is not beauty but predictability. Every block is roughly the same size, every address follows a logical numbering scheme, and a stranger can navigate the entire city with nothing more than two numbers. As a result, the grid transformed real estate into something that could be measured, bought, and sold like any other commodity — a rectangular lot has a knowable area, while an irregular medieval-style plot does not.

[¶3] However, the grid was not without critics, then or now. Diagonal avenues were later carved through the original plan precisely because a pure grid forces every trip into a longer, angular path — walking from one corner to its diagonal opposite takes longer on a grid than it would along a straight line. In contrast to the efficiency gained in navigation, some efficiency was lost in travel distance.

[¶4] Despite these tradeoffs, the grid's dominance was sealed by a single unglamorous virtue: it made a chaotic, fast-growing city legible. Emergency services could find an address; new residents could orient themselves within minutes; developers could calculate a lot's worth without visiting it in person. The grid did not need to be loved. It needed to be understood at a glance, by everyone, immediately.

[¶5] Students who study geometry are, in a small way, retracing that same commission's reasoning: measuring space, comparing shapes, and asking which arrangement of lines and angles solves a real problem most efficiently. The grid was never meant to be admired. It was meant to work.

Q1. The author includes the detail that "critics at the time called it monotonous and unimaginative" (¶1) primarily to: L1
Q2. What job does the sentence "a stranger can navigate the entire city with nothing more than two numbers" (¶2) perform? L2
Q3. In ¶3, the phrase "In contrast" signals a relationship of: L1
Q4. What is the function of the detail about diagonal avenues being "later carved through the original plan" (¶3)? L3
Q5. A student claims: "The list of examples in ¶4 — emergency services, new residents, developers — simply repeats facts about the grid that the reader already knows." Is this claim supported, partially supported, or contradicted? L4
Q6. What job does the final sentence, "The grid was never meant to be admired. It was meant to work" (¶5), perform in the passage as a whole? L3
