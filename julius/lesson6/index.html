Warm-Up · Quick Recall

Before We Move On: Prove It Stuck

Think first
Last week you learned the Pythagorean theorem. If a right triangle has legs 6 and 8, what's the hypotenuse? Say the rule out loud before you calculate.
Why this matters
Volume problems often hide a right triangle or a circle inside a 3D shape. If your 2D geometry is shaky, the 3D formulas won't save you — we're stacking new skills on old ones today.
Your Habit Target
You scored 0% on both Circles and Volume in Diagnostic A. Today isn't about memorizing more formulas — it's about understanding what each formula is actually counting. Say each formula in words before you plug in numbers.
Zone 1 · Circles Revisited

Every 3D Solid Starts With a Flat Shape You Know

Think about it
A cylinder is a stack of circles. If one circle has area πr², and you stack "h" copies of it, what would the total space inside be? Try to guess the formula before I show you.
Circle basics — the foundation for everything today
Circumference = 2πr    Area = πr²
Zone 2 · Rectangular Prisms

Volume Is Just "Base Area" Times "Height"

Think about it
Area of a rectangle is length × width — you already know this. Volume just adds one more dimension. What do you think happens if you multiply that flat area by how "tall" the box is?
V = l × w × h
Zone 2 · Practice

Rectangular Prisms — Easy to Hard

1Level 1
A box measures 5 cm by 4 cm by 3 cm. Find its volume. Ask yourself: which three numbers get multiplied?
2Level 3
A fish tank has volume 240 in³. Its base is 8 in by 6 in. How tall is the tank? Work backward from the formula.
SHSAT Trap: Mismatched Units
If one dimension is given in a different unit (say, feet instead of inches), convert everything to the same unit before you multiply — otherwise your answer will be off by a huge factor, not a small error.
Zone 3 · CylindersHigh-Frequency

Swap the Rectangle Base for a Circle

Think about it
A prism's volume is base area × height. A cylinder is really a "circular prism." What's the area of a circle again? Now substitute that in for the base.
V = πr²h
Zone 3 · Practice

Cylinders — Easy to Hard

3Level 2
A cylinder has radius 3 cm and height 10 cm. Find its volume in terms of π. What goes in for r, and what goes in for h?
4Level 4
A cylindrical water tank has diameter 14 ft and height 20 ft. Using π ≈ 22/7, find the volume. What's the trap hiding in "diameter"?
SHSAT Trap: Diameter vs. Radius
The single most common cylinder error is plugging the diameter straight into r² instead of dividing by 2 first. Always circle the word "diameter" or "radius" the second you see it in a problem.
Zone 4 · Cones & Pyramids — Stretch Goal

Shrink a Cylinder to a Point — Multiply by 1/3

Think about it
If you filled a cone with water and poured it into a cylinder with the same base and height, how many cone-fuls would fill the cylinder? (Hint: it's exactly 3.) What does that tell you about the cone's volume compared to the cylinder's?
The rule
A cone or pyramid always holds exactly 1/3 the volume of the prism/cylinder that shares its base and height. Compute the "full" shape's volume, then multiply by 1/3.
Cone: V = (1/3)πr²h   |   Pyramid: V = (1/3) × (base area) × h
Zone 4 · Practice

Cones & Pyramids — Easy to Hard

5Level 3
A cone has radius 6 in and height 9 in. Find its volume in terms of π. Compute the cylinder volume first, then apply the rule.
6Level 4
A square pyramid has a base edge of 8 m and height 12 m. Find its volume. What shape is the base, and what's its area formula?
SHSAT Trap: Forgetting the 1/3
Test-writers count on you forgetting the 1/3 factor entirely, or applying it twice by accident. Write "×1/3" as a separate step in your work — never fold it silently into your head math.
Zone 5 · Composite 3D Figures

Split It Into Shapes You Already Know

Think about it
Imagine an ice cream cone with a scoop on top — a cone plus a hemisphere. Would you add the two volumes together, or subtract one from the other? What if a cylindrical hole was drilled through a block instead?
The rule
Solids stacked/attached → add the volumes. Material removed (a hole, a notch) → subtract the volume. Always redraw the composite as two separate labeled shapes before computing anything.
Zone 5 · Practice

Composite Figures — Easy to Hard

7Level 4
A solid is made of a rectangular block 10 × 6 × 4 (in inches) with a cylindrical hole of radius 2 in drilled all the way through the height (4 in). Find the remaining volume. Set up both volumes separately before combining.
SHSAT Trap: Adding Instead of Subtracting
A drilled hole removes material — it should shrink your total, not grow it. If your composite-figure answer is bigger than the original block, re-check whether you should have subtracted.
Zone 5 · Accuracy

Three Ways to Check a Volume Answer

Worked example: cylinder, r = 3, h = 10 → V = π(3)²(10) = 90π
CheckBefore You Start
Sanity-Check the Shape
Does your final unit make sense? Volume should always come out in units³ — if you get plain units or units², you skipped a dimension somewhere.
CheckAfter You Finish
Estimate With π ≈ 3
Swap π for 3 quickly: 90π ≈ 270. If your calculator answer is wildly different from that estimate, you likely mis-squared the radius or forgot a factor.
CheckIf Time Allows
Redo the Formula From Scratch
Rewrite the formula on a blank line and re-substitute your numbers without looking at your first attempt. Getting the same answer twice, independently, is strong proof it's correct.
SHSAT Trap: Confusing Volume With Surface Area
Volume measures what's inside a solid (units³); surface area measures its outer skin (units²). Before you pick a formula, ask: "am I filling this shape or wrapping it?"
ELA · Poem Structure

Structure Isn't Decoration — It Builds Meaning

Think about it
If a poet suddenly shortens every line in the final stanza, what feeling might that create — rushing, calm, tension? Why might a poet repeat the same phrase three times across a poem?
What to look for
  • Stanza breaks often mark a shift in topic, time, or feeling.
  • Line length changes can speed up or slow down the poem's pace.
  • Repetition emphasizes an idea or builds emotional intensity.
  • Always ask: "why did the poet make this structural choice here?"
ELA · Practice

Spot the Structural Choice

8Level 2
A poem repeats the line "the tide comes in" at the start of each stanza. What is the most likely effect of this repetition on the reader?
9Level 3
In the final stanza, the poet switches from long, flowing lines to short, one-word lines. What effect does this shift most likely create?
SHSAT Trap: Naming the Device Without Explaining Its Effect
Identifying "repetition" or "short lines" earns you nothing on its own. Every structure question is really asking: what effect does this have on tone or meaning? Always finish your answer with "...which makes the reader feel ___."
