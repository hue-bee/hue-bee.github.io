The Unsteady Line

1 By four o’clock, the rain garden behind the public library had become a patchwork of dark soil and silver puddles. Samira crouched beside the measuring post, balancing the ecology club’s tablet on one knee. The screen showed a thin blue line for soil moisture—steady all week, then plunging straight down at noon.

2 “That can’t be right,” she said. In twenty minutes, the neighborhood council would arrive to hear whether the garden was preventing storm water from flooding the sidewalk. Samira had promised Dr. Chen that the graph would be ready.

3 Eli leaned over her shoulder. “The other sensors look normal. Leave this one out.”

4 The suggestion was practical. One bad line could distract the council from three months of good results. Still, Samira remembered installing this sensor herself. She had pushed the metal probe into the soil until it met resistance, then forced it another inch because the others sat deeper. If the probe had cracked, the strange line belonged to her.

5 “Give me five minutes,” she said.

6 She checked the wireless connection first. Strong. She compared the noon readings from the nearby rain gauge. Ordinary. Finally, she followed the sensor cable beneath the wet leaves. Halfway to the post, the cable vanished under the wooden border of a newly moved planter.

7 Samira lifted one corner. The cable underneath was flattened, not broken. When Eli shifted the planter aside, the blue line on the tablet trembled and began to rise.

8 “So we keep the graph?” Eli asked.

9 Samira looked toward the library doors. Through the glass, adults were folding umbrellas and greeting Dr. Chen. The corrected reading would not erase the plunge already stored on the screen.

10 “We keep all of it,” she said. “But we label the bad interval and explain what happened.”

11 Eli frowned at the crooked line. “Won’t that make the project look unreliable?”

12 Samira wiped a stripe of mud from the cable. A minute earlier, the graph had seemed like a verdict on her work. Now it looked more like a map: not perfect, but useful if its marks were read honestly. “It shows we checked,” she said.

13 During the presentation, a council member pointed to the sudden drop exactly as Eli had feared. Samira explained the pinched cable, the tests they had performed, and the label they had added. For a moment, the room was quiet.

14 Then the council member nodded. “So the garden is working, and now your monitoring plan is better too.”

15 On the tablet, the new blue line continued across the screen—not smooth, but moving in the right direction.
