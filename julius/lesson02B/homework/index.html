Passage Set 1 — The Moth Map

1 When Mira first volunteered for the county’s moth survey, the recording sheet looked almost empty: date, time, temperature, location, and a few pale marks where an insect had landed. She wondered how such small observations could matter. The coordinator, Dr. Han, did not promise a dramatic discovery. “Make the same kind of note every time,” she said.

2 Volunteers placed white cloths beside porch lights for twenty minutes after sunset. They photographed each visitor, recorded the weather, and stopped at the same time each night. That routine mattered because a count made for two hours could not be fairly compared with one made for twenty minutes.

3 By midsummer, hundreds of modest entries formed a map. A volunteer’s photograph of a brown moth with a silver hook on each wing appeared repeatedly near wetland edges but almost nowhere else. Researchers then checked those locations and found that the moth’s caterpillars depended on a plant common in damp soil.

4 The map also shaped a decision at the public library. Survey nights showed heavy moth activity in its small native-plant garden. The library replaced an uncovered security lamp with a shielded fixture that directed light downward. Later counts suggested that more moths remained near the garden instead of circling the bulb.

5 Yet the map’s blank spaces were as revealing as its clusters. Few observations came from apartment buildings, where residents had no porch on which to hang a cloth. The group created window-sized survey kits and partnered with tenant associations. The map grew more complete—not because any one observation became spectacular, but because many people learned to record ordinary things in the same careful way.

Passage Set 2 — The Narrow Route

1 The chalk line along Willow Street bent around tree pits, cellar doors, and one stubborn newspaper box. Lena, walking behind Mr. Ortiz with a clipboard, thought the line looked less like a route than a mistake someone had tried to erase.

2 Their class was measuring whether the block left enough clear sidewalk space for wheelchairs and strollers. At each obstacle, two students stretched a tape measure across the narrowest point while Lena recorded the width. When someone declared a spot “obviously too tight,” Mr. Ortiz tapped the clipboard. “Numbers first, opinions second.”

3 Outside a bakery, stacked flour deliveries reduced the open path to thirty-two inches. As the students wrote, a parent approached with a stroller. One wheel struck a bag, and the parent had to back up into the stream of pedestrians. The number on Lena’s sheet no longer felt like a mark for an assignment; it described the exact pinch point everyone had just watched.

4 At lunch, Malik suggested that they measure how long the deliveries remained outside. If the bags blocked the route for only five minutes, he argued, a permanent barrier might solve the wrong problem. The group returned the next morning and timed the unloading. They also sketched a loading zone that would preserve a forty-eight-inch path.

5 When the students presented their proposal, Lena began with the crooked chalk line. This time she did not see a mistake. She saw a question that their first measurements had sharpened and their second round of evidence had answered. Before sitting down, she asked the transportation committee one more thing: could the class measure the revised path after a trial week?
