Front-Loaded Opener: Statistics Stays Alive

CAT script
Start the timer. Narrate what the question is asking. At 90 seconds, eliminate, commit, and move forward—no return.
1Level 3
The mean of five quiz scores is 82. Four scores are 76, 81, 84, and 89. What is the fifth score?
2Level 4

Socratic debrief
For #1, what total do five scores need? For #2, which measure uses every value—and therefore “feels” the outlier?
0:05–0:12 · Zone 1

Units Are Not Labels—They Are Algebra

Think before computing
If the answer must be in miles, which unit must survive? Where should hours appear so they disappear? Can a numerical answer be right if its unit is wrong?
The UNIT GPS
Circle destination → write units → cancel → compute → sanity-check
Clever explanation: “Build a cancellation machine.”
Put each conversion fraction in the direction that destroys an unwanted unit. If a unit refuses to cancel, your machine is pointing the wrong way.
0:12–0:19 · Zone 2

Rate × Time: Let the Units Predict the Operation

Think about it
A speed is miles per hour. If hours must vanish and miles must remain, should time multiply or divide? Write the units before the numbers answer for you.
(miles/hour) × hours = miles
Two-leg trip rule
Build each leg separately, then add distances. Never average rates unless the problem asks for an average rate—and never average them blindly.

SHSAT trap: incompatible clocks
Hours and minutes cannot cancel each other. Convert first. Ask: “What clock is the rate using?”
0:19–0:22 · Zone 3

Unit Price: Make Every Package Speak the Same Language

Think like a translator
“$6.30 for 18 ounces” and “$8.40 for 24 ounces” are not directly comparable. What common phrase could both packages say—dollars per ounce or ounces per dollar?
unit price = cost ÷ amount
Best buy = lower dollars per same unit
Clever shortcut
If both unit prices are awkward, cross-multiply: compare 6.30 × 24 with 8.40 × 18. Same denominator, no decimals required.

Scale Drawings + Maps

Scale Is a Translation Dictionary

Ask which world you’re entering
If 1 inch on a map means 8 miles in reality, does a 3.5-inch route require multiplication or division? How do the labels “map” and “real” prevent reversing the scale?
3.5 map in × (8 real mi / 1 map in) = 28 real mi
Clever explanation: “Picture world → real world.”
Place the world you want on top. Your answer unit should be visible before you touch the calculator—or, on SHSAT day, your pencil.

0:25–0:32 · Zone 4

“Per” Is a Fraction Bar: Read the Denominator

Where does the “per” unit live?
If a city has 4,800 people per square mile, is square mile in the numerator or denominator? To find people in 2.5 square miles, what must cancel?
4,800 people/mi² × 2.5 mi² = 12,000 people
The denominator is the instruction manual
“People per square mile” tells how many people belong to one square-mile chunk. Count the chunks; multiply by the amount in each.

0:32–0:36 · ELA Method

A Detail Is a Bolt—Ask What Machine It Helps Build

The wrong question
“What does this sentence say?” only paraphrases. The stronger question is: “Why did the author spend space on this detail, and which larger claim does it help prove?”
“This detail shows _____, which supports the author’s point that _____.”
No frame, no answer choice
Predict the bridge first. Then reject choices that are true but too narrow, too broad, or disconnected from the passage’s purpose.

Passage: When a City Measures Its Heat

Tool:
Color:

1On a July afternoon, two New York City blocks can feel as if they belong to different climates. A tree-lined block may be several degrees cooler than a nearby stretch of dark pavement. To understand these differences, community groups have begun conducting “heat walks,” carrying small sensors along fixed routes while recording shade, surface type, and time.

7The measurements do more than confirm that summer is hot. They reveal where heat collects and when the problem becomes most severe. Because the walkers repeat routes at the same hours, planners can compare one location with another instead of relying on scattered impressions.

12One team found especially high readings near a bus stop surrounded by asphalt and lacking a shelter. The stop served many older residents from a nearby apartment complex. After the group presented its map, the neighborhood requested a shade structure and trees—not merely a general promise to “address heat.”

19Heat maps are not perfect. Sensors can differ slightly, wind can change during a route, and a single walk cannot represent an entire season. Yet repeated measurements turn a vague complaint into evidence that officials can examine, question, and act upon. In that sense, the most important product of a heat walk is not a colorful map; it is a more precise public conversation.

The “Why” Behind the Hardest Moves

Math chain (#19): 60 bottles
Choice B is the only chain ending in bottles. 2.4 × 7.5 = 18 L; 18,000 ÷ 300 = 60. Units verify the setup before arithmetic.
Scale area (#13): 540,000 m²
Actual sides are 900 m and 600 m. Multiply after converting: 900 × 600 = 540,000.
ELA #24: honest limits strengthen the claim
The concession prevents overclaiming, then explains why repeated evidence remains useful.
ELA #25: product vs. purpose
The map is the product; a more precise public conversation is the larger purpose. The best answer preserves that distinction.
