Warm-Up · Quick Recall

Before We Move On: Prove It Stuck

Think first
What does 23 actually mean — is it 2 × 3, or is it something else entirely? Write out the full expansion before you answer. What role does the exponent play versus the base?
Why this matters
Counting Principle earned 0% on your last diagnostic — but it's really just multiplication with a story attached. Exponents are also just multiplication, repeated. Today, two "impossible" topics turn out to be the same idea twice.
Your Habit Target
Today isn't about memorizing rules as magic tricks. It's about seeing why am × an = am+n works, and *why* multiplying choices — not adding them — counts outcomes correctly. Say the reasoning out loud before you compute.
Zone 1 · Exponent RulesHigh-Frequency

Exponents Are Just Counting Repeated Factors

Think about it
Write out 23 × 22 as repeated multiplication of 2's. Count how many 2's you have in total. Now compare that total to 3 + 2. Does that pattern always hold when you multiply same-base powers?
The three core rules — same base only
am × an = am+n    am ÷ an = am-n    (am)n = amn
Zone 1 · Practice

Exponent Rules — Easy to Hard

1Level 1
Simplify 32 × 34. Are the bases the same? If so, what do you do to the exponents?
2Level 2
Simplify 57 ÷ 53. Which exponent gets subtracted from which — does order matter here?
3Level 3
Simplify (23)4. Careful — this is a power raised to a power, not two powers multiplied. What rule applies?
4Level 4
Simplify (2x3y2)2 × x. What happens to the coefficient 2, and to each variable's exponent, when you square the whole group first?
SHSAT Trap: Different Bases Can't Combine
You can only add or subtract exponents when the bases match exactly. 23 × 32 stays as two separate powers — it does NOT become 65.
Zone 2 · Negative & Zero Exponents

A Negative Exponent Means "Flip It," Not "Make It Negative"

Think about it
Look at this pattern: 23=8, 22=4, 21=2, 20=?, 2-1=?. Each step down, you divide by 2. Continue the pattern — what must 20 equal? What must 2-1 equal as a fraction?
a0 = 1  (a ≠ 0)    a-n = 1/an
Why this makes sense
Dividing by the base each time you decrease the exponent by 1 is exactly the quotient rule in action: 21 ÷ 21 = 20 = 1, and 20 ÷ 21 = 2-1 = 1/2.
Zone 2 · Practice

Negative & Zero Exponents — Easy to Hard

5Level 1
Evaluate 70. What does the zero-exponent rule say, regardless of the base?
6Level 2
Evaluate 4-2 as a fraction. Flip the base into the denominator first, then apply the positive exponent.
7. Level 3: Simplify 3-1 × 34. Which combined exponent, and then which value, results?
8Level 4
Simplify (2-3 × 25) ÷ 2-1. Combine the numerator's exponents first, then handle the division by a negative exponent — does dividing by a negative exponent add or subtract?
SHSAT Trap: Negative Exponent ≠ Negative Number
A negative exponent never makes the final value negative (as long as the base is positive). 2-3 is a small positive fraction (1/8), not -8.
Zone 3 · Square & Cube Roots

A Radical Is an Exponent Question in Reverse

Think about it
√49 is asking: "what number, multiplied by itself, gives 49?" What number is that? Now ∛27 asks the same question but with three copies multiplied together — what number works there?
Build your mental toolkit
Perfect squares: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, 121, 144. Perfect cubes: 1, 8, 27, 64, 125. Memorizing these turns radical problems from "figure it out" into "recognize it instantly."
Zone 3 · Practice

Square & Cube Roots — Easy to Hard

9Level 1
Evaluate √81. What number times itself lands exactly on 81?
10Level 2
Evaluate ∛125. Which small number, cubed, equals 125?
11Level 3
Simplify √72. Find the largest perfect square factor of 72 first — what stays under the radical after you pull it out?
12Level 4
Evaluate √50 + √18. Simplify each radical to the same "leftover" root first — can you then combine them like like-terms?
SHSAT Trap: You Can't Add Unlike Radicals Directly
√50 + √18 is NOT √68. You must simplify each radical first to see if they share the same leftover root — only then can you combine coefficients, just like adding like terms.
Zone 4 · Counting Principle — 0% Zone Rebuild

Counting Outcomes Is Multiplication in Disguise

Think about it
You have 3 shirts and 2 pairs of pants. For each shirt, how many pants could you pair with it? Now list every outfit combination by hand — how many total outfits are there? Compare that count to 3 × 2.
The rule
If one event can happen in m ways and a second independent event can happen in n ways, then both events together can happen in m × n ways. For three or more independent choices, multiply all the counts together.
Total outcomes = (choices for step 1) × (choices for step 2) × ...
Zone 4 · Practice

Counting Principle — Easy to Hard

13Level 1
A diner offers 4 sandwich choices and 3 drink choices. How many different sandwich-and-drink meals are possible? What two numbers do you multiply?
14Level 2
A license plate has 2 letters followed by 3 digits. If letters and digits can repeat, how many plates are possible? (26 letters, 10 digits.) How many "slots" are there total?
15Level 3
A 4-digit code uses digits 0–9, and no digit may repeat. How many codes are possible? Careful — does every slot still have 10 choices?
16Level 4
A pizza shop offers 5 toppings, and a customer picks exactly 2 different toppings (order doesn't matter, e.g., pepperoni+onion is the same as onion+pepperoni). Why doesn't the plain counting principle 5 × 4 give the right final answer here — and what fix is needed?
SHSAT Trap: "No Repeats" Shrinks Later Choices
When an item can't be reused, the number of options for each later slot drops by one each time. Don't multiply the same number for every slot unless the problem explicitly allows repeats.
Zone 5 · Mixed Practice

Mixed Number Sense — Spot the Skill

Think about it
The SHSAT often blends exponents, radicals, and counting in one question. Before solving, ask: "is this about repeated multiplication of one number, or about multiplying independent choices together?"
17Level 4
A game uses a 6-sided die rolled 3 times, and each roll's result is recorded in order. If the number of possible sequences equals 6n, what is n, and what is the total number of sequences? Then: is 63 itself a perfect square, a perfect cube, both, or neither — and how do you know?
SHSAT Trap: Solving the Wrong Sub-Problem First
Multi-skill problems reward slowing down before writing anything. Circle the exact quantity the question asks for last — it's easy to solve for a middle step and stop there.
Zone 5 · Accuracy

Three Ways to Check a Number Sense Answer

Worked example: 4 sandwiches × 3 drinks = 12 meals
CheckBefore You Start
Sanity-Check Exponent Direction
Ask: should this exponent make the number bigger (positive) or smaller (negative/fraction)? If your answer moves the wrong direction, recheck your rule.
CheckAfter You Finish
List a Few Outcomes By Hand
For small counting problems, physically list 3–4 outcomes to confirm your multiplication logic matches reality before trusting a big number.
CheckIf Time Allows
Square Your Radical Answer
For a simplified radical like a√b, square the whole expression and confirm it equals the original number under the root.
SHSAT Trap: Confusing "Add the Choices" With "Multiply the Choices"
Add choices only when events are alternatives ("either/or," pick one path). Multiply choices when events happen in sequence ("this AND then that"). Before you answer, ask: "am I choosing one path, or building a combo of several steps?"
ELA · Fiction Passages

What a Character Does Reveals Who They Are

Think about it
If a character "slammed the door and refused to answer texts for a week," what does that action suggest about how they feel — even though no emotion word is used? What single detail is your strongest evidence?
The annotation approach
  • Underline what the character says, circle what they do.
  • Motivation = the "why" behind an action — find it in context, not just the dictionary definition of a word.
  • Theme is the takeaway that could apply beyond this one story — ask "what lesson does this character's arc teach?"
  • Tone is the author's attitude — look at word choice, not just plot events.
ELA · Practice

Motivation, Theme, or Tone?

18Level 2
A character "practiced her speech in the mirror every night for a month before the competition." What does this reveal about her motivation?
19Level 3
A story ends with a character who cheated finally confessing and losing his trophy, but feeling "lighter than he had in months." What theme does this ending suggest? Use one phrase as evidence.
SHSAT Trap: Confusing Plot Summary With Theme
"He lied, then he told the truth" is a plot summary, not a theme. A theme is the broader lesson — like "honesty brings relief, even when it costs something." Push one level deeper than what literally happened.
ELA · Practice, Continued

Two More Fiction Checks

20Level 1
A passage says: "She laughed loudly at every joke, even the ones that weren't funny." What tone does this detail create around her character? Point to the exact phrase that supports your answer.
21Level 4
A boy "gave his last dollar to a stranger, then walked home hungry, smiling the whole way." Which is the stronger interpretation: he regrets the choice, or he values generosity over comfort? Defend your choice using the specific details given — not just a gut feeling.
Habit Check
Before locking in a motivation, theme, or tone answer, ask: "could I point to the exact sentence that supports this?" If not, it's probably too big a leap — go back and find your evidence first.
