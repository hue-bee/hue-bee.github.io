Why Bridges Don't Fall Down

[¶1] In 1940, the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in Washington State collapsed just months after it opened — not because it was too weak, but because engineers had not accounted for how wind could make the whole structure twist and oscillate. The failure became one of the most studied events in engineering history. As a result, every bridge built since has been tested not only for weight, but for motion.

[¶2] Modern bridges rely on a small set of core ideas to stay standing under enormous stress. Trusses — networks of triangles — distribute weight evenly because a triangle is the only shape that cannot be pushed out of form without bending one of its sides. Suspension cables convert the downward pull of gravity into tension that travels up to giant towers and down into the earth. In addition, expansion joints allow a bridge to grow and shrink slightly with temperature changes without cracking.

[¶3] However, no amount of clever design matters if a bridge is not inspected regularly. Steel corrodes, concrete cracks, and bolts loosen over decades of traffic and weather. Engineers use sensors, drones, and manual inspections to catch small problems before they become emergencies. Consequently, most bridge failures today are traced not to bad design, but to deferred maintenance.

[¶4] My cousin once tried to build a bridge out of spaghetti and marshmallows for a science fair, and it held exactly four pennies before collapsing. It was a fun afternoon, but it taught him that even a small structure needs a plan before you start stacking material onto it.

[¶5] Understanding these principles is not just for engineers. Every commuter who crosses a bridge is trusting a century of accumulated lessons about triangles, tension, and inspection schedules. The next time you drive over one, remember: it isn't standing by accident. It is standing because someone solved a very old set of problems in a very deliberate way.
