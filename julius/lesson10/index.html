Warm-Up · Quick Recall

Before We Move On: Prove It Stuck

Think first
If you solve x + 3 = 7, you get x = 4 — one exact answer. If instead you solve x + 3 > 7, why should the answer be an entire range of numbers instead of just one? What does that tell you about what ">" actually means?
Why this matters
Inequalities sit at 33–50% across both diagnostics — right in the middle, which usually means the algebra itself is fine but one specific rule (the sign flip) is inconsistent. Today we isolate that exact moment and drill it until it's automatic.
Your Habit Target
Every time you multiply or divide both sides of an inequality, say out loud: "Am I multiplying or dividing by a negative? If yes, flip the sign now, before I forget." Build the habit into the step itself, not as an afterthought.
Zone 1 · One- & Two-Step InequalitiesHigh-Frequency

Solving Inequalities Is Solving Equations With a Range

Think about it
Solve 2x + 3 < 11 exactly as you'd solve 2x + 3 = 11: subtract 3 from both sides, then divide by 2. What do you get? Since you never multiplied or divided by a negative, does the inequality symbol change at all?
The core process — same as equations
Add/subtract to isolate the x-term → divide by the coefficient
Why this makes sense
Adding or subtracting the same number from both sides never changes which side is bigger — the gap between them just shifts. That's why those two moves never require flipping the sign.
Zone 1 · Practice

One- & Two-Step Inequalities — Easy to Hard

1Level 1
Solve x − 5 > 2. Which single operation isolates x, and does it require a sign flip?
2Level 2
Solve 3x + 4 ≤ 19. What do you do first — subtract, or divide? Why does order matter here?
3Level 3
Solve 5 − 2x < 13. Careful — after you subtract 5, what sign is attached to the x-term, and how does that change your next step?
4Level 4
Solve 2(x + 3) − 4 ≥ 3x − 1. What must you do before you can combine any x-terms? Where do the variables end up relative to each other?
SHSAT Trap: Distributing Before Combining
Multi-term problems require distributing across parentheses before you combine like terms. Skipping this step is the single most common source of wrong answers in Level 4 inequality problems.
Zone 2 · Flipping the Sign33–50% Zone

Multiply or Divide by a Negative? Flip the Sign

Think about it
Start with the true statement 2 < 5. Multiply both sides by −1. You get −2 and −5 — which one is actually bigger on a number line? Is "−2 < −5" still a true statement, or do you need to flip the symbol to keep it true?
If a < b and c < 0, then ac > bc
Why this makes sense
Multiplying by a negative flips every number to the opposite side of zero, like a mirror. The number that was farther right becomes farther left — so the order of "bigger" and "smaller" reverses too.
Zone 2 · Practice

Flipping the Sign — Easy to Hard

5Level 1
Solve −x > 6. What are you dividing by to isolate x, and what happens to the symbol?
6Level 2
Solve −3x ≤ 12. Divide both sides by −3 — remember the rule from the diagram. What's the resulting inequality?
7. Level 3: Solve −2x + 5 > −7. Isolate the x-term first — then decide whether the final division step needs a flip.
8Level 4
Solve 4 − 3x ≥ 2x − 6. If you move the x-terms to the side where the coefficient stays positive, do you ever need to flip the sign at all? Try both approaches and compare.
SHSAT Trap: Forgetting the Flip on the Final Step
Test-writers love burying the negative division as the very last move. Even if you handled every earlier step correctly, one missed flip at the end gives the exact reversed (and wrong) answer choice — which is usually listed as a trap option.
Zone 3 · Graphing on a Number Line

Open Circle, Closed Circle, and Which Way to Shade

Think about it
For x > 3, is 3 itself part of the solution? For x ≥ 3, is 3 part of the solution? What visual difference should that produce at the point 3 on the number line — filled in, or hollow?
< and > = open circle (not included)    ≤ and ≥ = closed circle (included)
Why this makes sense
The arrow always shades toward every number that makes the inequality true. For x > 3, plug in 4 — true, so shade right. Plug in 2 — false, confirming you shade away from it.
Zone 3 · Practice

Graphing Inequalities — Easy to Hard

9Level 1
Describe how to graph x ≥ −2. Open or closed circle at −2? Which direction does the shading go?
10Level 2
Solve and graph x + 4 < 9. What's the boundary point, and which type of circle matches "<"?
11Level 3
Solve and graph −2x ≤ 10. After you divide by a negative and flip, which way does the final arrow point?
12Level 4
A number line shows an open circle at −3 with shading to the right. Write the inequality it represents. Then, if the circle were closed instead, how would the written inequality change?
SHSAT Trap: Shading the Wrong Direction
Don't just look at which way the inequality symbol "points." Plug in one test number from each side of the boundary point to confirm which direction actually makes the statement true — symbols can mislead you after solving.
Zone 4 · Compound Inequalities

One Line, Two Conditions at Once

Think about it
The statement −2 < x < 5 is really saying two things at the same time: x is greater than −2, AND x is less than 5. If you split it into those two separate inequalities, what do you notice they share in common?
−2 < x < 5  ≡  x > −2 AND x < 5
Why this makes sense
Whatever you do to solve, you must do to all three parts of the compound inequality — the left side, the middle, and the right side — to keep every piece balanced together.
Zone 4 · Practice

Compound Inequalities — Easy to Hard

13Level 1
Graph 1 ≤ x < 6. Which circle at 1 is closed, and which circle at 6 is open?
14Level 2
Solve −3 ≤ x + 2 ≤ 7. What operation do you apply to all three parts to isolate x?
15Level 3
Solve 4 < 2x + 2 ≤ 10. After subtracting 2 from all three parts, what's your next move, and does it require a flip?
16Level 4
Solve −6 ≤ −2x + 4 < 8. Isolate the x-term in the middle first — then, when you divide by −2, what happens to the entire compound inequality, including which side each boundary ends up on?
SHSAT Trap: Flipping Only One Side
When you divide a compound inequality by a negative, both inequality symbols flip, and the two boundary values swap positions (the smaller one moves to the left). Forgetting to swap positions is the most common Level 4 error.
Zone 5 · Mixed Practice

Word Problems — Translate Before You Solve

Think about it
Before writing any symbols, ask: does the phrase mean "at least," "at most," "more than," or "fewer than"? Each phrase maps to exactly one inequality symbol — get the translation right first, and the algebra becomes routine.
17Level 4
A parking garage charges $5 to enter plus $3 per hour. Julius has at most $23 to spend. Write an inequality for the number of hours h he can park, then solve it. Should the boundary hour value itself be included in the answer — and how do you know from the phrase "at most"?
SHSAT Trap: Skipping the Translation Step
Jumping straight to numbers without writing the inequality symbolically first is how "at least" and "more than" get mixed up — and that single symbol error changes whether the boundary value is included.
Zone 5 · Accuracy

Three Ways to Check an Inequality Answer

Worked example: solved x < 4 — pick a test point to confirm
CheckBefore You Start
Count Your Negative Multiplications
Scan the whole problem first and count exactly how many times you'll divide or multiply by a negative — that's how many flips your final answer should have.
CheckAfter You Finish
Plug In a Test Number
Pick one number that satisfies your final answer and substitute it into the original inequality. If it doesn't work there, you missed a flip somewhere.
CheckIf Time Allows
Match Circle Type to Symbol
Before finalizing a graph, re-read the symbol: < and > must be open circles, ≤ and ≥ must be closed. A quick symbol-to-circle glance catches most graphing slips.
SHSAT Trap: Trusting the Symbol Without Testing It
The fastest way to catch a missed flip is substitution — it takes ten seconds and catches almost every sign error. Build it into your routine on every inequality problem, not just the hard ones.
ELA · Revising/Editing Part 2

Every Sentence Must Earn Its Place

Think about it
If a paragraph is about the benefits of recycling, and one sentence suddenly describes a student's favorite pizza topping, what's wrong with that sentence — even if it's grammatically perfect? What question can you ask of every sentence to catch this?
The three moves in Part 2
  • Transitions: words like "however," "therefore," "in addition" signal the logical relationship between ideas — pick the one that matches the actual logic, not just one that "sounds right."
  • Topic sentence revision: a good topic sentence previews every idea the paragraph actually discusses — no more, no less.
  • Off-topic sentences: ask "does this sentence support the paragraph's one main idea?" If not, it gets cut, regardless of how well-written it is.
ELA · Practice

Transitions & Topic Sentences

18Level 2
"The team practiced every day for a month. ___, they lost the first game." Which transition word correctly signals the contrast between effort and outcome?
19Level 3
A paragraph discusses three causes of coral bleaching, but its topic sentence only mentions "rising ocean temperatures." Rewrite the topic sentence so it accurately previews all three causes.
SHSAT Trap: Choosing a Transition That "Sounds Fancy"
Words like "furthermore" and "consequently" are often wrong-answer traps for students who pick based on tone rather than logical relationship. Always ask: is this addition, contrast, cause-effect, or example? Match the category, not the vocabulary level.
ELA · Practice

Eliminating Off-Topic Sentences

20Level 1
Paragraph: "Bees pollinate roughly a third of the food crops we eat. They travel from flower to flower collecting nectar. My cousin is allergic to bee stings. Without bees, many fruits and vegetables would become far more expensive." Which sentence should be removed, and why does it not belong?
21Level 4
A paragraph's main idea is "why sleep improves memory." One sentence reads: "Some people prefer to study in the morning rather than at night." Is this sentence off-topic, or does it subtly support the main idea? Justify your answer using the exact wording of the main idea.
SHSAT Trap: Confusing "Related Topic" With "Supports the Main Idea"
A sentence can be about the same general subject and still be off-topic if it doesn't support the paragraph's specific claim. Always re-read the exact main idea sentence before judging — not just the general topic.
