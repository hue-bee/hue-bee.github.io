0:05–0:13 · Core Tool

Put on Multiplier Goggles

Ask one question
After the change, what fraction of the original remains? Convert that “after fraction” directly to a decimal multiplier.
WORDS
increase by p%
→
MULTIPLIER
1 + p/100
→
ACTION
original × multiplier
WORDS
decrease by p%
→
MULTIPLIER
1 − p/100
→
ACTION
original × multiplier
Clever check: multiplier weather report
A multiplier above 1 predicts growth; a multiplier between 0 and 1 predicts shrinkage. If the answer moves the wrong direction, stop before submitting.
Percent Change

The Base Is the Measuring Stick

Which amount earns the 100% label?
When a quantity changes from old to new, why must the change be compared with the old amount rather than the new one?
percent change = change ÷ original × 100%
Same gap, different percent
The same numerical increase can represent different percent changes when the starting amounts differ. Name the base before dividing.

0:13–0:18 · Successive Changes

Two Discounts, Two Different Bases

Why not add?
After the first discount, is the second percent acting on the original price or the smaller sale price? What changed between the two steps?
final = original × m₁ × m₂
Multiplier train
Each percent change is a train car. Couple the multipliers in order. Do not flatten them into one added percent unless you first multiply the cars together.

0:18–0:22 · Opposite Directions

Markup Then Discount: A Seesaw With a Moving Pivot

Equal percentages, equal effect?
If a price rises and then falls by the same percent, is the decrease taken from the original price or the larger marked-up price? Which move acts on more dollars?
cost × markup multiplier × discount multiplier
Trap: “they cancel”
Opposite percent changes cancel only when their combined multipliers equal 1. Check the product, not the wording.

0:22–0:26 · Nested Groups

Percent of a Percent: Zoom Into the Subgroup

Who is inside whom?
If 40% of a group has Trait A and 25% of that subgroup has Trait B, are you finding 25% of everyone or 25% of the 40% slice?
whole × subgroup percent × nested percent
Camera zoom
The first percent frames the scene; the second zooms inside that frame. Multiplication preserves the nesting.
0:26–0:29 · Reverse Percent

Run the Machine Backward

What operation undoes multiplication?
If the final amount equals the original times a known multiplier, how can you recover the original from the final amount?
original = final ÷ multiplier
Trap: subtracting the percent from the final
The stated percent was taken from the unknown original, not from the final amount. Rebuild the equation before choosing an operation.

Math Accuracy

The B-M-S Check

BBase
Who owns the percent?
Point to the exact amount that represents 100% at this step. If it changes, rename it.
MMultiplier
Which direction?
Above 1 grows; between 0 and 1 shrinks. Reverse problems divide by the known multiplier.
SSize
Does the result fit the story?
Estimate first. A discount should not increase price; a nested percent should be smaller than its parent group.
Five-second final question
If I hid my arithmetic, could I still predict whether the answer should be larger or smaller than the starting amount? If not, I have not modeled the story yet.

Fiction Passage · Part I

The Model City Lines 1–18

1By four o’clock, the library’s basement smelled of cardboard, wet paint, and the peppermints Mr. Vale kept in his coat pocket. Eli stood over the neighborhood model he had promised to finish for Saturday’s history exhibit. Tiny brick buildings surrounded a strip of blue cellophane river. At the center, the old train station waited beneath a desk lamp, its paper clock frozen at 6:12.

6“You made the windows too even,” Mr. Vale said from the doorway.

7Eli looked up. “That’s what rulers are for.”

8Mr. Vale set down a box of photographs. In one, the station leaned slightly toward the tracks, as if listening for a train. One second-floor window was narrow; another had been patched with darker brick. “Buildings remember mistakes,” he said. “Perfect copies forget them.”

13Eli disliked advice that arrived dressed as a riddle. The model had already taken three afternoons, and the exhibit card carried his name alone. He imagined visitors bending close, searching for a crooked wall or a smear of glue. Still, he lifted the roof and examined the neat row of identical windows.

Pause without answering
What does Eli want people to see in his work? What does Mr. Vale want the model to preserve instead?
Fiction Passage · Part II

The Model City Lines 19–38

19At the bottom of the box, Eli found a photograph dated 1978. A crowd filled the platform. Near the edge stood a girl holding a handmade sign: DON’T CLOSE OUR STOP. Beside her, a boy no older than Eli held the sign’s other end. The boy was Mr. Vale.

24“They closed it anyway?” Eli asked.

25“For eleven years.” Mr. Vale unwrapped a peppermint but did not eat it. “People kept writing letters. Kept meeting. Eventually, the trains came back.”

29Eli studied the photograph again. The sign sagged in the middle, and the painted letters crowded one another. It was badly measured. It was also the first thing his eyes returned to.

33He reached for his craft knife. Instead of replacing the station wall, he narrowed one window and added a patch of darker brick beneath it. Then he cut a sign no wider than his thumb. The letters would not fit if he made them perfectly even.

37“Do you have a red pencil?” he asked.

38Mr. Vale smiled and slid one across the table.

Trace the shift
Which object changes Eli’s idea of accuracy? Does he abandon precision, or does he redefine what an accurate model should include?
