The Trail That Moved

1Jules held the folded trail map at chest height, although the wind kept snapping its corners against her jacket. “The overlook is twenty minutes east,” she announced. She had volunteered to lead the younger hikers because she knew this park—or had known it last summer, before the wildfire closed half the ridge.

6Rafi pointed toward a new wooden sign. Its arrow aimed downhill. “That says Cedar Loop.”

9“Temporary detour,” Jules said. She tucked the map under her arm before he could study it. On the page, Cedar Loop curved uphill exactly where the blackened slope now opened below them.

14They continued east until the path ended at a shallow stream. Across the water, no trail emerged—only ferns and a row of pale stones. The group's conversation thinned to the small noises of boots shifting on gravel.

20Jules unfolded the map slowly, as if a slower motion might change what it showed. The printed trail crossed no stream. “The storm must have moved the markers,” she said.

25“Or the rangers moved the trail,” Rafi replied. He was not teasing now. He crouched near the bank and brushed mud from one of the pale stones. A stripe of blue paint appeared.

31The map legend marked blue as the new route. Jules felt every face turn toward her. She could insist that the paper was right, or she could lead them somewhere.

36“Show me the next marker,” she said, handing Rafi the map.

39They found another blue stripe under a fallen branch, then another on the far side of the stream. Jules tested each stone before anyone crossed. Rafi called out the marks; she chose the safest footing. The overlook appeared fifteen minutes later, bright beyond the charred trunks.

47At the railing, Jules smoothed the useless map against her knee. She drew the stream in pencil and added three blue dots. “For the next guide?” Rafi asked.

52“For me,” Jules said. Then she left room for whatever the trail might change next.
