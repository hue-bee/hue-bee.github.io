A Record of the River

Tool:
Color:

[¶1] The student Watershed Club had recorded an eight-minute description of a river survey. It named three sampling locations and explained how water was collected. When the club prepared a public listening station, Jo suggested placing a printed transcript beside the headphones. Jo wanted to find out what the extra text might help listeners do, rather than simply assuming that more material was better.

[¶2] Twenty-four visitors volunteered for a trial during one afternoon. Twelve chose to hear the recording alone; twelve chose to hear the same recording with the transcript available. Each visitor then answered the same questions without help. Seven visitors in the audio-only group correctly named all three sampling locations. Ten visitors in the audio-plus-transcript group did so.

[¶3] The volunteers used a shared scoring rule: a response counted as complete only if it named all three locations. The recording, headphones, and question sheet were unchanged between conditions. The team did not record how long anyone took to finish, and visitors could replay the audio as often as they wished.

[¶4] Jo also wrote down an observation that the totals could not capture. One listener heard the unfamiliar word “tributary,” found it in the transcript, reread the sentence around it, and then pointed to the smaller stream on the station’s map. “Seeing the word helped me follow that part,” the listener explained. This described one listener’s use of the text, not the experience of every visitor.

[¶5] The club’s adviser warned against calling the difference a clear effect of the transcript. Visitors had chosen their own condition instead of being randomly assigned. In a brief background question, more people in the transcript group said they already knew the river than in the audio-only group. Prior knowledge could therefore help explain the difference in complete answers.

[¶6] For the next trial, the club planned to assign conditions by drawing slips, record prior familiarity, and measure completion time separately. Jo kept a careful first conclusion: the transcript group had more complete location answers in this trial, and at least one listener described a useful way to consult the text. Whether the transcript itself caused the difference, or made the task faster, remained open.

Original fictional classroom passage. All people, events, and counts are invented for this activity; they are not a report of real research.

The Sketches in the Costume Box

Tool:
Color:

[¶1] Noor found costume sketches in a school theater box labeled “Spring play.” A digital archive would be easier to search if every image had a year. But supplying a convenient date and establishing a defensible date were different tasks.

[¶2] Of the 42 sketches, eight carried clearly written dates from 2011 and eleven carried dates from 2012. The other 23 had no readable date. Noor counted a sketch as dated only when a year appeared on the sheet itself. Similar pencil lines or positions near dated sheets did not meet that rule.

[¶3] One dated 2012 sketch showed a coat for the character Mira. Beside its sleeve was the instruction “Widen this seam; Mira must lift both arms in the final scene.” A second dated drawing of that costume stated, “After the sleeve revision, the actor can reach overhead without pulling the coat upward.” The notes linked a particular alteration to a performance need.

[¶4] Other notes pointed toward different priorities. An undated drawing had a gold border and the instruction “Make this edge visible from the back row.” Another sheet showed a flower pattern without explaining its purpose. Noor could record the pattern, but assigning a reason required more evidence.

[¶5] Arden, a former student, remembered revising cuffs after a dress rehearsal but could not identify the related sheets. A surviving 2012 program pictured a coat resembling Mira’s. Noor treated it as evidence about that costume, not a date stamp for the whole box.

[¶6] A classmate proposed labeling every undated sketch “2012” because many resembled the dated drawings, and describing all alterations as comfort improvements. Noor objected: the box might mix several productions, and the notes showed that visibility could matter alongside movement.

[¶7] The finished archive separated “written on the sheet” from “inferred from another source.” Some dates remained blank, and explanations named the particular drawing they described. Readers could see where the record ended and interpretation began.
