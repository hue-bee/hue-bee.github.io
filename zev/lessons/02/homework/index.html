1 On Saturday morning, Elena’s grandfather handed her the key to his repair shop and stayed on the sidewalk. “You know where everything is,” he said. Until then, Elena had swept floors, sorted screws, and watched him coax clocks back into rhythm. She had never opened the shop alone.

2 Inside, the silence seemed louder than the ticking clocks. Elena raised the shades and arranged three repaired radios on the counter. When the bell above the door rang, Mr. Han entered carrying a lamp whose switch had jammed.

3 Elena almost called her grandfather. Instead, she asked Mr. Han what had happened, unplugged the lamp, and tested the cord. The switch contained a bent metal tab—small enough to straighten, but easy to snap. She remembered her grandfather’s warning: “Force hides the problem; patience shows it.”

When Sidewalks Became Test Sites

1 City planners once treated storm drains mainly as pipes that moved rainwater away from streets. During intense storms, however, water often reached the drains faster than underground systems could carry it. Some cities have begun testing “sponge blocks”: streets redesigned to hold water briefly where it falls.

2 A sponge block may combine tree beds, shallow planted channels, and pavement that lets water seep through. None of these features stores an entire storm. Together, though, they slow runoff, giving drains more time to work and reducing the sudden surge that can flood intersections.

3 The approach also creates questions. Planted channels require maintenance, and permeable pavement can clog with dirt. Results differ by soil, slope, and rainfall pattern. For that reason, engineers monitor each test block rather than assuming one design will work everywhere.

4 Even an imperfect pilot can be useful. A block that absorbs less water than expected may reveal where sediment gathers or which plants survive road salt. In this way, the street becomes not only part of the solution but also a place where the next solution is measured.
