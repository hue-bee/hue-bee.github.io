When a Map Stops Helping

Original informational practice passage. The study is fictional and is not a report of scientific findings.

[¶1] Knowing where a place is does not always mean knowing how to reach it when the usual road is closed. A map may support both tasks, yet a person who remembers isolated landmarks may still struggle to connect them. A community-center team wondered whether reconstructing a map from memory would help people use those connections, rather than merely recognize familiar locations.

[¶2] In an imagined study, adult volunteers who had never seen a tabletop model town were randomly assigned to two groups. Everyone studied the same labeled map for the same amount of time. The maps were then removed. One group drew the town from memory; the other traced a faint street outline. Neither group could see place labels during this activity. Both groups worked for an equal period and received the same instructions about the later tests.

[¶3] Immediately after the activity, one test asked volunteers to place named landmarks on an unlabelled map. A second presented a blocked road and asked them to choose the shortest available route between two sites. The first task assessed remembered locations; the second required using relationships among streets. The examiners who scored the responses did not know which activity each volunteer had completed.

[¶4] The memory-drawing group performed better on the blocked-road task, while the groups differed little on the landmark-placement task. When volunteers took new versions of both tests a month later, the route-choice advantage was still present but smaller. The team did not treat these patterns as interchangeable: remembering names and positions was not the same outcome as finding an alternative path.

[¶5] The team also cautioned that the volunteers came from one adult community-center population, and a tabletop town is not a crowded city. Children, experienced drivers, and people navigating under pressure had not been tested. Nor had the researchers measured brain activity. Random assignment strengthened the comparison between the two activities; it did not make the participants representative of every possible map user.

[¶6] The result therefore supported a limited proposal: under these study conditions, reconstructing a map appeared useful for a task requiring flexible route choices. It did not establish that everyone should abandon printed maps, or that drawing improves every kind of memory. A useful conclusion, the team argued, should be no wider than the evidence carrying it.

E1. Which task in the study most directly required volunteers to use street relationships rather than recall landmark locations? L1
E2. What is the main function of the distinction in paragraph 1 between knowing a location and reaching it after a road closes? L2

Part D2 — Literary Inference & Structure

6 Questions ·

Read for the passage’s argument or development. Answer E7–E12; all six remain editable until the set-level commitment on the next page. Evidence notes are unscored.

The Window Has a Vote

Original literary practice passage. Type: the individual letters arranged for printing. Proof: a trial print used to check an arrangement.

[¶1] The first poster I set in my aunt’s print shop looked splendid from the stool. I had crowded the neighborhood fair’s name with every broad letter I could find. Stars filled the corners, and a border marched around the edge. When I reached the final line, however, the words had nowhere to stand. I pushed the letters closer, as if a crowd could be persuaded to shrink by being asked politely.

[¶2] Aunt Inez lifted the proof but did not move a single letter. “Where will they read it?” she asked. I pointed toward the window. She handed it back. “Then the window ought to have a vote.” I carried the sheet outside, embarrassed by what seemed an unnecessary errand. From the opposite curb, my splendid heading became a dark bar, and the fair’s date disappeared into the border.

[¶3] Back inside, I replaced the broad letters with narrow ones. Now every word fitted, but the poster looked tired and timid. I waited for my aunt to repair it. Instead, she slid a strip of scrap paper across the table and set the worn metal ruler beside it. “You can test one line without spending a whole sheet,” she said. She returned to the press, leaving the decision, but not the tools, with me.

[¶4] I tried the date on the strip. I spread the letters, then spread them too far. On the third attempt, I left a little room around the line instead of decorating it. Outside, I could read it without leaning forward. For the first time that afternoon, I was not asking whether I had filled the paper. I was asking whether a stranger could use it.

[¶5] My aunt placed a fresh sheet on the table—the good paper, not the practice stock. “Your arrangement,” she said. I removed two stars and most of the border. The blank spaces had a job now. They held the date apart from the address and gave each line somewhere to begin. I had expected to feel that I had made less of a poster; instead, I felt that I had finally made one.

[¶6] The last border still leaned. My aunt laid the ruler along it, and I reset the type myself. She had not stopped noticing mistakes, and I had not stopped making them. But when the poster went into the window, I watched a passerby pause, read the date, and point it out to a friend. I looked back toward the press. My aunt was already working, though she had left the door open.

E7. What did the narrator observe from the opposite curb on the first trip outside? L1
E8. What does the aunt’s suggestion to test one line on scrap paper most strongly suggest about her teaching? L2
