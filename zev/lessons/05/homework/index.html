The Quiet Track

Original fiction written for this activity

[¶1] Omar had promised to make a recording of the birds that visited his apartment building’s roof. He pictured a clean track: a sparrow’s whistle, a pause, another whistle. On Saturday he set his borrowed recorder beside a planter and waited. Just as a bird called, a delivery truck rattled below. Omar stopped the recording.

[¶2] For twenty minutes he kept starting over. A gate clicked. Someone dragged a chair across a balcony. At last he lowered his headphones. “There isn’t a quiet minute,” he told Mrs. Chen, who was watering the herbs. She nodded toward the sparrow. “It seems to have found one.”

[¶3] Omar listened without the headphones. The sparrow’s call rose between two bursts from a drill. He had heard only the drill before. Now he noticed that the bird repeated its short pattern, pausing when another bird answered from the fire escape.

[¶4] He made a new recording and let it run. A chair scraped; the sparrow continued. Later, indoors, he almost cut out a section in which a bus hissed at the corner. But just after the hiss, a second kind of bird gave a thin, descending call. He moved his finger away from the delete key.

[¶5] For the recording’s label, Omar wrote “Roof Birds, Saturday Morning.” He added the time, the weather, and a note about the street sounds. He could not identify the second bird yet. He left a blank space for its name instead of removing its call.

[¶6] The next morning he returned with the recorder and a small notebook. Downstairs, a truck door slammed. Omar wrote the time and pressed record.

The Long Way Round

Original fiction written for this activity

[¶1] Leena’s first map to the neighborhood open house was a single bold arrow. It led from the bus stop over the footbridge and straight to the school gym. “Six minutes,” she told her brother, who was helping deliver supplies. On the page, the route looked satisfyingly simple.

[¶2] At the bridge, her brother stopped the wagon carrying their boxes. Twelve steps rose to the walkway. They carried the boxes up one at a time, then returned for the wagon. By the time they reached the gym, a corner of Leena’s map had become soft in her hand.

[¶3] “We can write ‘stairs’ beside the bridge,” her brother said. Leena pictured a visitor arriving with a stroller and learning about the steps only after leaving the bus. She turned the map over. “Let’s try the park entrance before we finish this.”

[¶4] The park route curved around a fence and took several minutes longer. It also had a smooth ramp, a wide gate, and a crossing with a signal. Leena timed it with the loaded wagon. At the gate she stopped to sketch a landmark: a drinking fountain shaped like a silver question mark.

[¶5] Back at the gym, a volunteer glanced at the revised map. “Why send people the long way?” Leena placed the first draft beside it. “This one measures the walk,” she said, touching the old arrow. Then she pointed to the new route. “This one tells people whether they can make it.”

[¶6] She kept the footbridge on the final map, labeled its stairs, and added the park route with its landmarks. The page was less tidy now. Before sending it to the printer, Leena checked that both arrows began at the bus stop.
