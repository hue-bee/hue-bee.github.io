## SLIDE 3 — Same Student, Two Opposite Trends
**Time: 0:05–0:09 · Zone 1**

This is the emotional hinge of the session. Slow down.

### Step 1 — Read the bars, not the feelings
> "ELA: 64.9 percent correct in March, 49.1 in June. That's down almost 16 points. Math: 45.6 in March, 63.2 in June. Up more than 17. Same student, same three months, same brain. One half went up more than the other half went down."

### Step 2 — The Socratic question (on the slide)
> "If nothing about your intelligence changed between March and June — and it didn't — what actually did change?"

**Wait. Full silence. Count to eight.** This question does the whole job if you don't rescue her from it.

**Likely answers and your responses:**

- *"I studied math more."* → "Yes. Say the second half of that sentence." (Target: *and I didn't study ELA at all.*) Then: "So the math number is proof your study method works. It's not luck — you moved 17 points with it. That's the good news hiding in a bad-looking report."
- *"I got worse at reading."* → "Can a person get worse at reading in twelve weeks without a head injury? What's more likely — that reading left you, or that the specific *question types* were never practiced?"
- *"I don't know, I guessed more?"* → "Interesting. Why would you guess more in June? Time pressure? Which means the fix is a speed habit, not a reading habit. Hold that thought — we'll test it against your subscores in one slide."
- Silence → offer the narrow version: "Did you spend equal time on both halves?"

### Step 3 — Land the reframe (read the win-card)
> "Here's the sentence I want you to actually take home: a volatile score isn't random — it's a map. The math gain proves the method works when you apply it. The ELA drop and the arithmetic slump both point at skills that went *untouched*, not skills you can't learn. Those are completely different problems and only one of them is scary. Yours isn't the scary one."

### Tutor note
If she gets discouraged here, the counter is arithmetic, not comfort: *"You gained 17.6 points on one half in one cycle. We have nine weeks. Do the multiplication."*

## SLIDE 5 — Every Paragraph Has a Job to Do
**Time: 0:13–0:19 · Zone 2 (Paragraph-Job Annotation) · CORE SKILL**

This is the highest-value six minutes of the session. Do not rush the Socratic beat.

### Step 1 — The Socratic question (on the slide)
> "If a paragraph didn't exist, would the passage be missing *information*, or would it be missing a *reason*? What's the difference between a paragraph that gives facts and one that persuades?"

**Wait.** Then a concrete follow-up if she stalls:
> "Imagine a passage about wolves returning to Yellowstone. One paragraph lists how many wolves and what year. Another paragraph says 'critics argued the program would endanger ranchers.' Delete the first — what's lost? Delete the second — what's lost?"

Target: the first loses data; the second loses the *argument's structure* — you no longer know there was a disagreement being resolved.

### Step 2 — The rule
Point at the formula box.
> "Read paragraph → write a three-to-six-word job → move on. Three to six words. Not a summary. Not a sentence. A job title."

Give the vocabulary of job labels explicitly — she cannot label what she has no words for. Write these where she can see them:

- introduces the problem
- gives background
- gives an example
- gives a counter-example
- raises an objection
- rebuts the objection
- explains a cause
- shifts to a new stage / time period
- summarizes and calls for action
- defines a term

> "Ten labels. Ninety percent of nonfiction paragraphs are one of these."

### Step 3 — Read the diagram
> "Look at the stack. Para 1 introduces the problem. Para 2 gives a counter-example. Para 3 rebuts, then concludes. Notice you can understand the *shape* of that whole passage without knowing what it's about. That's what structure questions test."

### Step 4 — Why this fixes her exact gap
> "Purpose and structure questions are really asking 'what job did this paragraph do?' If you already named the job in the margin, you're not re-reading under time pressure — you're just checking your own notes. That's the difference between a 23 percent and a real score. Not more reading. Notes."

### Tutor note
Anticipate the objection *"doesn't that take extra time?"* Answer: *"Six words per paragraph, maybe eight seconds. A single re-read of a paragraph costs you thirty. You're buying time, not spending it."*

## SLIDE 6 — Naming the Job: Easy to Hard
**Time: 0:19–0:31 · Zone 2 · Practice**

Have her type into the textareas — the boxes exist so the work is visible. **She answers first, every time.** You talk after.

### Problem 1 (Level 1)
*"Many scientists once believed the Earth was flat." In 3–6 words, what job is this paragraph doing?*

**Target:** "sets up an outdated belief" / "gives a belief to be corrected" / "background before a contrast."

**Coaching:** the word doing the work is **once**.
> "Why 'once'? What does that single word promise you about the next paragraph?"

Target: that the belief was abandoned — a contradiction is coming. If she wrote *"talks about the flat Earth"* — that's the trap on this slide arriving early. Flag it and hold it; you'll name it formally at the end of the slide.

### Problem 2 (Level 2)
*"However, recent studies suggest the opposite is true." Name its job and explain how "however" gave it away.*

**Target:** contradicts / overturns the previous paragraph's claim. The clue is that *however* is an adversative transition — it exists only to reverse direction.

**Coaching:**
> "Transitions come in a few flavors. Some add ('furthermore', 'in addition'). Some sequence ('then', 'next'). Some show cause ('therefore', 'as a result'). And some reverse ('however', 'yet', 'nevertheless', 'on the other hand'). You can name a paragraph's job from its first three words more often than you'd believe. Circle transitions on sight."

Push once further:
> "If Para 2's job is 'contradicts Para 1,' what does that tell you Para 1's job must have been?"

Target: to state the claim being contradicted. This is the moment she sees jobs come in *pairs* — that's the structural insight.

### Problem 3 (Level 4, multiple choice)
*Final paragraph restates the author's claim and adds "so what should be done about it." Which job label fits best?*

**Answer: B — Summarizes and calls for action.**

**Wrong-answer autopsy, do all three:**
- **A, introduces a new counter-argument** — "Can a *final* paragraph introduce something new? What does that violate about how nonfiction is built?" Final paragraphs close; they don't open.
- **C, provides a neutral historical fact** — kill the word *neutral*. "Is 'here's what should be done' neutral? A recommendation has a position."
- **D, defines a technical term** — no definition is present. This is the distractor that describes a real job type, just not this paragraph's. "Real label, wrong paragraph" is a distractor pattern she'll see all season.

> "And notice the tell: 'so what should be done about it.' That phrase *is* the call to action. The question handed you the answer in the stem. Structure questions do that more often than you expect."

### Step 4 — The SHSAT trap (read it aloud)
> "'What is this paragraph about' is not the same as 'what is this paragraph doing.' A paragraph can be *about* bees while its *job* is to contradict what the last paragraph claimed about them."

Then close the loop:
> "Here's your homework filter for the next week. Every job label you write — check whether it's a topic or a verb. If your label has no verb in it, it's a topic and it's worthless. 'The flat Earth' is a topic. 'Sets up a belief to correct' is a job."

### Tutor note
If she's fast and you finish at 0:27, don't advance early. Pull any nonfiction paragraph from a practice book and have her label three in a row, aloud, under ten seconds each. Reps here are worth more than reps anywhere else in this deck.

## SLIDE 7 — Two Very Different Kinds of Wrong
**Time: 0:31–0:35 · Zone 3 (Careless vs. Conceptual) · DIAGNOSTIC**

### Step 1 — The Socratic question
> "If you got a problem wrong, could you have solved a nearly identical one correctly five minutes later? If yes — was the *rule* ever really the problem?"

**Wait.** Then:
> "That question is the entire diagnostic. Two kinds of wrong. Conceptual means you didn't know the rule. Careless means you knew it and executed wrong. They look identical on a score report and they need opposite fixes."

### Step 2 — Why the distinction is worth money
> "Say you miss six questions. If five are careless, more practice problems will barely help you — you'll just make the same slips on new problems. You need a *habit*, which is Zone 5. But if five are conceptual, a habit does nothing and you need re-teaching. Most students guess which one they are. Guessing wrong costs you an entire prep cycle. That's arguably what happened to your March-to-June math number in one direction and your ELA number in the other."

### Step 3 — The self-interview question (read the info card)
> "Here's the exact sentence to ask yourself on every single miss: *if I explained the rule to myself right now, could I re-solve this from scratch?* Yes means careless — unit conversion, decimal slip, sign error. No means conceptual, and that gets re-taught, not just re-drilled."

### Step 4 — Read the diagram as a flowchart
> "Wrong answer splits two ways. Conceptual, left: fix is re-teach from scratch. Careless, right: fix is a double-check habit. Different branch, different medicine. Nobody fixes a decimal slip by re-learning decimals."

### Tutor note
Watch for a real failure mode here: students label everything careless because "careless" feels less embarrassing than "I didn't know it." Pre-empt it.
> "Fair warning — 'careless' is the flattering answer, so people over-pick it. The honesty test: if I asked you to state the rule out loud *right now*, could you? If you can't state it, it wasn't carelessness. There's no shame in conceptual. Conceptual is the *easier* one to fix — I can teach a rule in four minutes. Habits take weeks."

## SLIDE 8 — Classify the Miss: Easy to Hard
**Time: 0:35–0:41 · Zone 3 · Practice**

She classifies first, out loud or typed. You respond after.

### Problem 4 (Level 1)
*A student wrote 3.5 ft = 350 in. Should be 42 in. Careless or conceptual? Name the exact cause.*

**This is the best teaching moment in the deck. Play it deliberately.**

Whatever she answers, ask:
> "Defend it. What would have to be true inside that student's head for your label to be right?"

Then hand her the actual method:
> "You can't classify this one from the paper. You need one follow-up question: *how many inches are in a foot?* If the student says twelve — careless. They knew the factor and reached for a decimal-shift habit, multiplying by 100 because that's what conversions usually feel like. If the student says 'a hundred?' — conceptual. Same wrong answer, opposite fixes. So the autopsy is never just staring at the mistake. It's one question aimed at the cause."

Note the connection to the next slide:
> "On the error log slide you'll see a row almost exactly like this labeled 'careless — wrong factor.' That's a judgment call somebody already made, and it's a defensible one, because ×100 instead of ×12 is a habit misfire. But you should know the label came from a follow-up question, not from the page."

Correct work, for completeness: 3.5 × 12 = 42 in.

### Problem 5 (Level 2)
*Solve 4.2 − 1.75. If a student lined up the decimals wrong and got 4.15, careless or conceptual? Then find the correct answer.*

**Answer: careless. Correct value 2.45.**

**Coaching:**
> "Why is that careless and not conceptual?"

Target: subtraction was known; place-value alignment was an execution slip.

> "And look at the size of the error. They got 4.15 from a problem whose answer is 2.45 — they subtracted almost nothing from 4.2. Which means an estimate would have caught it instantly. Four minus almost two is about two, not about four. Remember that. It's Zone 5 arriving early."

Optional: have her write 4.20 − 1.75 stacked, with the trailing zero. Name the micro-habit: *always pad to equal decimal places before you subtract.* That's a one-line error log entry.

### Problem 6 (Level 4, multiple choice)
*Converts 2.5 hours to 150 minutes, then adds 45 and gets 105 total. Which statement is TRUE?*

**Answer: B — the conversion was correct; the addition was careless; it should be 195.**

**Coaching — check the steps separately, in order:**
> "Step one: 2.5 hours × 60. Is 150 right?" (Yes.) "Step two: 150 + 45." (195.) "So where's the error, and what kind?"

> "Now — how do you get 105 from 150 and 45?"

Let her find it: they *subtracted*. That's the cause line for the log — "read + as −," an operation slip under speed, not a knowledge gap.

**Distractor autopsy:**
- **A, both conceptual** — fails because step one is flatly correct. "You can't call a right answer an error of any kind."
- **C, conversion careless / addition conceptual** — exactly inverted. Watch for her picking this by pattern-matching the words instead of checking the steps.
- **D, no error** — 105 ≠ 195.

### Step 4 — The SHSAT trap
> "Multi-step problems can be conceptually correct in one step and carelessly wrong in another. So the log tags *each step*, not just the final answer. If you write 'got it wrong, careless' for problem 6, you've logged nothing useful. You write 'step 2, careless, read plus as minus.' That sentence is fixable. 'Careless' alone isn't."

## SLIDE 9 — Build the Tool You'll Use All Season
**Time: 0:41–0:50 · Zone 4 (Error Log) · RUNS ALL 9 WEEKS**

This block is *doing*, not talking. Aim for four minutes of your voice and five of her writing.

### Step 1 — The Socratic question
> "If the same careless mistake shows up three weeks in a row, is more practice the fix — or is it a sign you need a specific new habit for that exact trap?"

Target: a habit. Push it:
> "Three weeks of the same slip means practice has already failed twice. Repeating a failed intervention is how people spend a whole season not improving."

### Step 2 — Read the log structure off the diagram
Four columns: `# | Problem Type | Careless/Conceptual | Cause`.

Walk the sample rows:
> "Row: problem 4, measurement, careless, wrong factor. Row: problem 5, arithmetic, careless, misaligned decimals. Row: problem 6, measurement, mixed — see step 2. That third one is the important row. 'Mixed' is a legal answer, and it points at the step."

### Step 3 — Now she fills it in, live
Have her log **problems 4, 5, and 6 from this session** into the real doc, in her own words. Then, and this is the point of the whole block, go to her June score report and log **every arithmetic and measurement miss** you can reconstruct.

For each one, ask exactly two questions:
1. "Could you state the rule right now?" → sets the careless/conceptual column
2. "What specifically went wrong?" → sets the cause column, in under twelve words

Keep the cause column brutal and specific. Reject vague entries out loud:
- ❌ "wasn't paying attention" → ✅ "dropped the negative when distributing"
- ❌ "rushed" → ✅ "used ×100 instead of ×12 on ft→in"
- ❌ "confused" → ✅ "didn't know perimeter vs. area formula" (and that one's conceptual)

### Step 4 — Why it matters (read the win-card)
> "This log is the single tool that turns Week 9's review into a targeted strike instead of a guess. Every entry today becomes evidence for what gets re-taught later. In Week 9 we're not going to *decide* what to review. We're going to *read* what to review, off this page."

### Step 5 — The maintenance rule
> "One row per miss, updated after every set. Not at the end of the week — you'll have forgotten the cause by then, and the cause is the whole value. The answer you can always look up. Why you missed it disappears in about an hour."

### Tutor note
If she resists ("this is a lot of writing"), reframe with the count: *"How many arithmetic misses are we talking — eight? Ten? Ten rows, twelve words each. That's the cheapest score improvement available to you, and it's the only one that compounds."*

## SLIDE 10 — Never Verify the Same Way Twice
**Time: 0:50–0:54 · Zone 5 (Check-Your-Work Rule)**

### Step 1 — The Socratic question
> "If you re-do a calculation the exact same way and you made a mistake the first time, will redoing it identically catch it? What has to change about your second pass?"

**Wait.** The realization has to be hers — this is the single most transferable idea in the deck.

Target: the *method* has to change, not just the attempt.

> "Right. A calculation error hides inside its own method. Your hand makes the same move, your brain has the same blind spot, and you get the same wrong number — and now you're *more* confident in it, which is worse than not checking. Re-doing identical steps isn't verification. It's repetition wearing a costume."

### Step 2 — The three second methods
Point at the formula box: **Estimate · Reverse operation · Plug back in.**

> "Three ways to check that aren't 'do it again.'
>
> **Estimate.** Round everything hard and see if you're in the right neighborhood. Catches every order-of-magnitude and decimal-place disaster. Costs five seconds.
>
> **Reverse operation.** Undo it. Subtracted? Add back. Divided? Multiply back. Catches arithmetic slips.
>
> **Plug back in.** Take your answer, put it into the original problem or the original units, see if the problem is satisfied. This is the strongest one for word problems and equations."

### Step 3 — Which method for which error
Make the matching explicit, because "check your work" is useless advice without it:
- decimal-place error → **estimate**
- sign or operation slip → **reverse**
- unit-conversion error → **plug back in** (convert the answer back and see if you land on the original)
- "does this even make sense" (a person's age is 340) → **estimate**

### Step 4 — Read the diagram's bottom box
> "All three agree? Lock it in. And here's the practical version for a timed test: you don't have time for all three on every problem. You do *one* second method on every problem, and you pick the one that catches the error type your log says you actually make. That's why the log comes before the rule."

### Tutor note
Connect it back to slide 3 explicitly — this is the payoff of the whole session:
> "Your March arithmetic was strong. Your June arithmetic was shaky. If that's careless — and your log now says it mostly is — this slide is the entire fix. Not more arithmetic. A second method, every time."

## SLIDE 11 — Solve, Then Verify: Easy to Hard
**Time: 0:54–0:58 · Zone 5 · Practice**

Rule for this slide, stated before she starts:
> "You don't get credit for the answer. You get credit for the answer *plus* the check. If you tell me a number without a second method, I'm going to ask 'how do you know?' and you're going to have nothing."

### Problem 7 (Level 1)
*A rope is 8.4 m. Convert to centimeters, then verify by estimating — is 840 cm roughly 8 meters?*

**Answer: 840 cm.** Check: 100 cm per meter, so 800 cm = 8 m exactly, and 840 is a bit more — consistent with 8.4. ✓

**Coaching:**
> "Notice how the estimate is built. You didn't re-multiply 8.4 by 100. You went the other direction and asked what 800 cm *is*. Different direction, same claim. That's what makes it a real check."

Then stress-test it:
> "What would 84 cm have told you?" (That you divided instead of multiplied — 84 cm is under a meter, nowhere near a rope's length.) "And 8400?" (Off by a factor of ten — 84 meters, longer than a building.) "The estimate catches both instantly. That's why it's five seconds well spent."

### Problem 8 (Level 2)
*Solve 17 − 5.65. Verify with the reverse operation — does your answer plus 5.65 return 17?*

**Answer: 11.35.** Check: 11.35 + 5.65 = 17.00 ✓

**Coaching:**
> "Watch the pad. Seventeen is 17.00, not 17. If you subtract without padding, you're back in problem 5's mistake — this problem is problem 5's twin, on purpose."

> "And note *why* the reverse check works here: addition is a genuinely different mental motion than subtraction. You aren't reusing the blind spot. Compare that to just re-subtracting, where you'd almost certainly repeat the same alignment error."

### Problem 9 (Level 4, multiple choice)
*Recipe needs 2.5 pints; cup shows ounces; 1 pint = 16 oz. Student pours 32 oz and calls it "close enough." Which check method proves this is wrong?*

**Answer: A — plug back in: 32 ÷ 16 = 2 pints, not 2.5. Short by 8 oz.**

**Coaching:**
> "Do the plug-back-in out loud. Take the answer, 32 ounces, and convert it back into the problem's original units. Two pints. The recipe wanted two and a half. So the answer isn't close-ish — it's short by half a pint, which is eight ounces. What should it have been?" (2.5 × 16 = 40 oz.)

**Distractor autopsy:**
- **B, no check needed since 32 is close to 2.5** — this compares a number in ounces to a number in pints. "Those aren't the same kind of thing. That's the whole trap. 32 and 2.5 aren't near each other; they're not even measuring in the same language."
- **C, estimating alone confirms 32 oz** — estimating never *confirms* a wrong answer. Estimation rules things out; it doesn't certify. Good moment to note that a check method can be real and still be the wrong tool for the job.
- **D, reverse operation not applicable to unit conversion** — flatly false, and worth killing, because reverse operation is one of her best tools *for* conversions. Multiplying by 16 to go pints→oz reverses by dividing by 16.

### Step 4 — The SHSAT trap
> "'Close enough' isn't a check. Eyeballing an answer *feels* like checking, which is exactly what makes it dangerous — it gives you the confidence of having verified without any of the verification. The rule is a second calculation, done a different way. Not a feeling about whether the number looks right."
